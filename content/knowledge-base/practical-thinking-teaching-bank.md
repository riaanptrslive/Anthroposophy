### 00 — Habit or a reason?

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 1–8. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Distinguish a familiar routine from a reason that can be examined.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

A community workshop always puts instructions beside the door. Visitors repeatedly ask where the tools belong. The organizer says the instructions must be clear because they have always been there.

#### Explanation of the example

Familiarity tells us that a practice has a history; it does not tell us whether it achieves its purpose. A useful reason connects a choice to an aim and to information we can check. In this example, questions from visitors are relevant evidence. They do not yet tell us whether the problem is wording, location, language or something else. A better opening is to ask what visitors actually encounter. Keep that distinction throughout this course: a plausible story is a starting point for inquiry.

#### Activity

For three short sessions, record one ordinary routine and the reason you give for it. State its aim and one observation that would challenge your reason. Use the workshop case if you prefer. Keep the first entry unchanged for Lesson 10.

#### Understanding question

What would you check before replacing the instructions?

#### Questions and explained answers

What would you check before replacing the instructions?

Watch where a visitor looks and ask what is unclear. Replacing the sign immediately would test a solution before identifying the problem.

#### Teaching conclusion

Distinguish a familiar routine from a reason that can be examined.

### 01 — An attitude of inquiry

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 9–13. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Distinguish spiritual creative activity from the human thinking that understands it, and connect this idea to careful observation.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

You designed a new sign. One visitor likes it; another still cannot find the tools. You are tempted to record only the compliment.

#### Explanation of the example

Feeling pleased with your work need not stop careful inquiry. The important question is whether that feeling decides which observations count. Write down the difficulty as accurately as the compliment. Understanding an objection does not require agreeing with it in advance. Steiner gives this attitude a spiritual foundation in the assigned passage: thoughts belong to reality, rather than only to the thinker. That is his philosophical claim. Our practical task examines how we respond to an unwelcome observation; its outcome cannot by itself establish that spiritual foundation.

#### Activity

Return to yesterday’s routine. On three occasions, record your first feeling, one observation you welcome and one you find inconvenient. Ask the same clarifying question of both. Finish by separating what you felt from what you learned.

#### Understanding question

Does openness mean accepting every criticism?

#### Questions and explained answers

Does openness mean accepting every criticism?

No. It means giving a criticism a fair hearing and checking its grounds. Neither liking nor disliking it settles whether it is accurate.

Does thinking about a plant make it grow in this passage?

No. Steiner attributes nature’s creative activity to spiritual beings. Our thinking can understand relationships in the growth; imagining the plant does not cause that growth.

What does the watchmaker example explain, and what further step does Steiner take?

The maker arranges parts according to relationships that another person can understand by studying the watch. Steiner extends this comparison to nature and calls it the work of spiritual beings; the watch example alone does not establish that extension.

How does this idea guide observation practice?

In §§ 9–12, Steiner presents the world as understandable through thought. In §§ 14–16, he asks for accurate observations of successive conditions before premature conclusions. The practice seeks to discover relationships without treating an assumption as a fact.

#### Teaching conclusion

For Steiner, human thinking can discover a spiritual order already active in the world. Investigate observed relationships before imposing your first interpretation.

### 02 — Observe before explaining

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** GA 108 §§ 14–18; GA 4, chapter VI §§ 4–6, in the linked English translations.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Distinguish percept, mental picture and concept; understand where the astral body enters Steiner’s interpretation.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

You observe a broad cloud with a bright edge. You look away, recall its shape, and then look again. The edge looks less distinct. You wonder whether the cloud changed, whether your memory was incomplete, and what could explain the difference.

#### Explanation of the example

Separate the present percept from the mental picture connected with your earlier observation. Compare concrete details before deciding what caused the difference. Understanding a change does not require already knowing its cause. Steiner’s account of the astral body is a spiritual interpretation of this activity, given in GA 108 §§ 17–18.

#### Activity

On three occasions, observe clouds from a comfortable place or use a familiar object. Note three details, look away and recall them. Look again and record a match or difference, allowing for the object having changed. Distinguish what you observed, remembered, understood and still do not know. Finally, explain Steiner’s astral-body interpretation separately. This immediate comparison is a teaching adaptation; in GA 108 §§ 14–16 Steiner proposes comparing observations on successive days.

#### Understanding question

How would you distinguish the percept, mental picture and spiritual interpretation in this example?

#### Questions and explained answers

How would you distinguish the percept, mental picture and spiritual interpretation in this example?

The percept is the content of present observation. The mental picture retains its connection to the previously observed cloud. The effect on the astral body is Steiner’s spiritual interpretation; it is not another colour or shape observed in the cloud.

#### Teaching conclusion

Observe carefully, recall faithfully, and distinguish a relationship you understand from a cause you merely suppose.

### 03 — Follow a change

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 14–18. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Compare successive observations without filling gaps with certainty.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

At the first observation, a leaf is folded and presents a narrow surface. At the next, it is more open. You did not watch the interval between the observations.

#### Explanation of the example

Preserve both states before proposing a cause. Compare outline, position and visible surfaces. As you relate the pictures mentally, distinguish the observed change from an imagined transition. At this stage you do not need to explain why it occurred.

#### Activity

On three occasions, observe the same plant or accessible natural process. Record the time and three concrete details. Place the records side by side and describe a change, a continuity and something still unknown. Do not invent intermediate stages or require yourself to give a cause. Compare with §§ 14–18 before moving on to prediction.

#### Understanding question

Is imagining the transition between two observations the same as having observed it?

#### Questions and explained answers

Is imagining the transition between two observations the same as having observed it?

No. Preserve the observed states and identify the transition as imagined. The comparison can be precise even without a causal explanation.

#### Teaching conclusion

Compare successive observations without filling gaps with certainty.

### 04 — Make a prediction you can check

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 19–21. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Use an outcome to revise an expectation.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

You move the sign beside the tool shelf and predict that fewer visitors will ask where things belong.

#### Explanation of the example

A prediction becomes useful when you state what would count as its outcome before observing it. “The workshop will improve” is too vague. “In the next comparable session, fewer visitors will ask where to return tools” can be checked, though visitor numbers and experience also matter. A correct prediction does not prove your explanation: several explanations may predict the same result. An incorrect prediction helps locate a missing condition. Record both outcomes without rewriting your original expectation.

#### Activity

Make one low-stakes prediction about your own routine on each of three occasions. Write the expected result, its reason, the actual result and one changed condition. Use a familiar task rather than predicting another person’s private intentions.

#### Understanding question

If questions decrease, has the sign been proved effective?

#### Questions and explained answers

If questions decrease, has the sign been proved effective?

It is encouraging evidence, but check attendance and prior experience. Repeat under reasonably comparable conditions before drawing a stronger conclusion.

#### Teaching conclusion

Use an outcome to revise an expectation.

### 05 — Investigate how something happened

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 22–26. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Propose an earlier cause, seek evidence and revise your explanation.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

Yesterday the tray held six pencils; today it holds four. You imagine that two were borrowed, but have not checked.

#### Explanation of the example

The count is an observation; borrowing is a hypothesis. Look for a record, check relevant locations or ask someone who may know a neutral question. If you find the pencils in another drawer, revise the borrowing story. If evidence is missing, explain why the cause remains open.

#### Activity

On three occasions, choose a small, checkable event from your routine. Record what you know, one possible cause, another possibility and a check that could distinguish them. Make the available check and record the result before revising. Do not turn a hypothesis about someone into an accusation; keep the question open when evidence is unavailable.

#### Understanding question

What is still missing after imagining a coherent cause?

#### Questions and explained answers

What is still missing after imagining a coherent cause?

Evidence about what actually happened. A coherent story can be false; checking lets you correct it or retain uncertainty.

#### Teaching conclusion

Propose an earlier cause, seek evidence and revise your explanation.

### 06 — Choose and sustain a line of thought

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Adams English translation, § 28: choice of subject and transition to the memory exercise.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Deliberately choose a subject outside habitual concerns; distinguish this exercise from detailed recollection.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

During an afternoon break, your mind returns to an unfinished task. You decide to spend five minutes thinking about a building entrance you noticed that morning: the door’s position, the steps and the route people take inside.

#### Explanation of the example

The main change is how the subject is selected: you choose it deliberately instead of following the automatic worry. You do not need to discover a different meaning in the scene or remember every detail. When the unfinished task returns to mind, notice and resume your chosen subject. The next exercise addresses precision of recollection.

#### Activity

On three occasions, choose a quiet break. Name an ordinary subject outside your current concerns: an overlooked scene or a short reading passage. Give it up to five minutes; begin with less time if needed. Afterwards record the subject, one diversion and how you returned. The time of day is your choice, including before sleep if convenient. Assess your choice and return to the subject, without turning the exercise into a search for new meanings or perfect recall.

#### Understanding question

Is a distraction evidence that the exercise failed?

#### Questions and explained answers

Is a distraction evidence that the exercise failed?

No. Noticing and deliberately returning is part of the practice. Explain where the thought departed from the chosen question.

Must you review your day differently before going to sleep?

No. The essential task is to choose a subject outside habitual concerns and direct attention to it. No particular time or new interpretation of the past is required.

Does recalling a scene make the two exercises identical?

No. The same scene can serve both, but the first trains choosing and sustaining a subject; the second trains reconstruction of details.

Are the thirty minutes mentioned a required concentration time?

No. Steiner describes half an hour available for rest and suggests even just five minutes on the chosen subject.

#### Teaching conclusion

Choose and sustain the subject. Reinterpreting the past and practising at bedtime are not required.

### 07 — Build and check a mental picture

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Adams English translation, end of § 28 and §§ 29–32: memory and imaginative completion.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Reconstruct details of an encounter and distinguish what you remember, do not know and imagine.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

Without looking, you describe the tool shelf. You are certain the scissors are on the right; a check shows them on the left.

#### Explanation of the example

Confidence and accuracy are different things. A mental picture can combine remembered details with reasonable guesses. When you check, keep your original description visible and annotate it instead of replacing it. This reveals both omissions and additions. You may work in words, spatial relationships or a sketch; vivid visual imagery is not required for this course. The point is to make an account specific enough to compare with the object. The immediate object comparison used in this course is a checkable adaptation. In the source, Steiner starts with a past encounter and then proposes imaginatively completing missing details. The passage explanation above distinguishes that proposal from what you actually remember.

#### Activity

Observe a small arrangement of ordinary objects. Look away and describe it, marking uncertain details. Check and annotate. Repeat on three days with a slightly different arrangement. Compare the kinds of omissions, not just the number of correct details.

#### Understanding question

Should you fill an uncertain gap to make the picture complete?

#### Questions and explained answers

Should you fill an uncertain gap to make the picture complete?

Label it as a guess. A fluent description can hide uncertainty; a visible gap gives you something precise to check.

What changes from the preceding exercise?

Previously, the focus was choosing a subject and returning to it. Now it is reconstructing the details of an experience and noticing the quality and gaps of recollection.

Does Steiner prohibit invented details in this passage?

No. In §§ 30–32 he proposes imaginative completion while acknowledging the picture will be inaccurate. In this course we identify these additions as imagined so they are not confused with memories.

Is the advice to sleep on a decision this same exercise?

No. That belongs to comparing alternatives in §§ 33–36. The memory exercise does not require bedtime.

#### Teaching conclusion

Detailed recollection is a separate exercise. An imagined addition must remain identified as imagination.

### 08 — Compare, pause and decide

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 33–36; compare the cause-and-effect example in § 38.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Examine alternative actions, set the question aside and return with reasons for a provisional choice.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

The workshop can keep one large sign or put labels on the shelves. You prefer the sign but have not examined what each option involves. The choice can wait until tomorrow.

#### Explanation of the example

Write what each action would involve, what supports it and what remains uncertain. Set a time to return and take the pause. On returning, compare again and record your reason for choosing and a condition for review. Pausing thus follows preparation of the decision.

#### Activity

Use three entries: 1) set out two possible actions for an ordinary decision that can wait, with reasons and consequences; 2) after a defined pause, return and record what changed in your assessment; 3) after acting, examine the outcome and say whether you would revise the choice. These three moments are the course’s teaching structure. Do not delay an urgent decision just to fulfil the exercise.

#### Understanding question

What distinguishes pausing a decision from simply avoiding it?

#### Questions and explained answers

What distinguishes pausing a decision from simply avoiding it?

You prepare alternatives and reasons, set a return time and resume the assessment. Avoidance need not include those steps.

#### Teaching conclusion

Prepare the comparison before the pause; on returning, decide with reasons and acknowledge the limits.

### 09 — Review how your thinking changed

**Prior course source:** `practical-thinking.mjs`; **prior reference:** Paragraphs 38–48 for the conclusion; revisit §§ 1–36 for the exercises.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

**[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching]** Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

#### Learning objective

Use earlier notes to assess a specific change and choose further practice.

#### Example — instructor-created unless explicitly attributed

You reread your first claim that the workshop instructions were clear. Your later notes distinguish the sign’s wording, its location and visitors’ needs.

#### Explanation of the example

A useful review identifies a change you can show. “I think better now” is broad; “I now write the observation separately from my first explanation” points to a practice and a record. Compare an early entry with a later one of similar difficulty. More elaborate writing is not automatically more accurate. Name a remaining difficulty and choose one exercise to repeat. This course provides a method of self-review, not a score for intelligence or a guarantee of spiritual development.

#### Activity

Gather earlier entries. Identify an observation, a hypothesis, a check and a revision. Compare an early and a later entry of similar difficulty; state what improved and what remains difficult. Choose the exercise matching that difficulty from the course map and explain why. Make three more short attempts and report the result without presuming improvement. Also explain one spiritual claim from the lecture that these records alone do not establish.

#### Understanding question

How does this connect with The Philosophy of Freedom?

#### Questions and explained answers

How does this connect with The Philosophy of Freedom?

These practices give you experiences to examine. The philosophical course asks what thinking contributes to knowledge and free action; completing exercises does not establish its arguments.

#### Teaching conclusion

Use earlier notes to assess a specific change and choose further practice.
