# When a child makes a mistake: guidance, listening and repair

Editorial prototype · 20 September 2026 · Proposed Unit 5 of **Anthroposophy in Everyday Life: Understanding and Supporting Children**.

For parents and educators working with early school-age children. Estimated study time: 15–20 minutes, to be tested. This is an English prototype; a reviewed Brazilian Portuguese version belongs in the proposed first release.

**Learning aim:** explain how adult self-examination and a child's participation can inform a clear, responsible response to a mistake.

## An ordinary situation

A child has used materials another child was keeping for a project. Your first thought is, “They knew better.” You feel angry and want to settle the matter immediately.

The materials need attention. Before deciding what the event means about the child, you also need to understand what happened.

## Understand the connection

The Foundations course distinguishes **thinking, feeling and willing** as cooperating activities. Here, thinking forms an account of what happened; feeling registers anger, concern or disappointment; willing becomes the action you decide to take. A strong feeling can be acknowledged while you examine whether your explanation is justified. The aim is to bring understanding into the action.

The adult's own development is part of this work. Steiner's account of inner training includes reviewing experiences with distance from the immediate reaction. Our application is to notice an assumption and how it shaped our response. The full spiritual purpose of his account extends beyond managing a conversation. [Steiner, GA 10, Chapter 1, “Inner Tranquility,” §§18–24](https://rsarchive.org/Books/GA010/English/RSPC1947/GA010_c01.html).

Hermann Koepke's *Encountering the Self* provides the more specific parenting connection: acknowledge a concrete harm and allow the child to participate in imagining a repair. Koepke is a later anthroposophical author. The following dialogue is an original course scenario, not a quotation or guaranteed method. [Existing lesson and source context](../docs/encountering-the-self/lessons/03.html).

## Work through the scene

You say: “These materials were being kept for Ana's project, and some have been used. Tell me what you understood about this box.”

The child replies: “I thought it was the shared box.”

You notice the label is unclear. This changes your explanation. You can acknowledge your part while keeping the effect on Ana's project in view:

“I can see why that was confusing. I should have made the label clearer. Ana still needs materials for her project. Let's find out what is missing and work out what can be returned or replaced.”

The adult remains responsible for making the situation workable. The child's contribution should fit their abilities. Ana also needs a voice in what would help.

At home, the materials could be a sibling's saved craft supplies. In a classroom, the teacher may need to clarify shared storage for the group and ensure the affected learner can continue the activity.

## Choose and explain

Which opening helps you understand the situation while keeping responsibility visible?

1. “You are selfish. Replace everything.”
2. “These materials belonged to another project. Tell me what happened.”
3. Replace the materials yourself and say nothing.

**Feedback:** the second opening names the event and leaves room for information. The first turns an action into a judgment about the person before hearing their account. The third might restore the materials but leaves the misunderstanding and opportunity for participation untouched. Listening does not complete the repair; it helps you choose an appropriate next step.

Now change the facts: the child says, “I knew they were Ana's, but I wanted them.” What changes in your response? What remains?

**Feedback:** the unclear-label explanation no longer fits. Acknowledge the child's account, state the boundary and work through an achievable repair. Describing the action and its consequences remains useful. A reason for taking something does not remove its effect on another person.

## Try and revisit

Choose an ordinary, low-stakes misunderstanding, or use the fictional example. Write four short notes:

| Question | Your note |
|---|---|
| What happened that I can describe specifically? | |
| What explanation did I add, and what did I feel? | |
| What did the child or other participant help me understand? | |
| What response and repair fit what I now know? | |

Return to the note later. Record what you followed through on, how participants responded and what you would revise. You do not need to create a mistake or provoke conflict to practise. Fictional work is sufficient for course participation.

## Check your understanding

Explain thinking, feeling and willing in this scene, one sentence for each. Then explain why admitting the adult's mistake can coexist with a clear boundary.

A strong response distinguishes observation from assumption, shows how understanding changed, and connects the action with the actual situation. It credits Steiner's self-observation separately from Koepke's parenting discussion and the course's invented dialogue.

**Carry forward:** bring this note to the unit on turning an ethical intention into a workable deed. Whose needs does the repair include? What practical conditions does it require? How can each person participate?
