Before you begin

This is a course for adult learners with some familiarity with Anthroposophy. Theosophy is a useful foundation. One orientation and seven thematic lessons follow all seven lectures; source reading takes longer than the concise explanations here. Allow roughly 20–30 minutes for a guided session and return for the activity at your own pace.

Read the story as Steiner retells it, make your own first interpretation, examine his account, and revise with reasons. Personal agreement is not the assessment criterion.

Three voices to keep distinct

  1. The narrative as retold in this source.
  2. Steiner’s spiritual interpretation.
  3. Your explanation, question or alternative.

Connect with earlier study

  • Human being and cosmos through a myth — Ancient Myths reads Osiris and the Greek divine generations as images of the human being’s relationship with the cosmos. Compare that later interpretation with the account here: identify one shared question and one claim that the myth alone cannot establish.
  • An image and a claim of spiritual knowledge — The veil of Isis offers a comparison for the work of knowing. Use it to distinguish ordinary imagery from Steiner’s technical claim about Imagination. A compelling image may clarify a question without verifying a supersensible perception.
  • Individuality in a later lecture — Lecture IV of Ancient Myths connects spiritual individuality with its critique of heredity as an exclusive explanation. Compare this later spiritual account with the argument you have studied here. State what the philosophical argument establishes on its own and what the later lecture adds.
  • Stories that invite understanding — Ancient Myths asks how stories can nourish understanding beyond remembered definitions. Compare that educational question with Koepke’s story. Design one open question and a response that lets the learner revise an interpretation; keep the two authors’ claims distinct.

Source and scope

Rudolf Steiner, Ancient Myths: Their Meaning and Connection with Evolution. Seven Dornach lectures, 4–13 January 1918, from GA 180; M. Cotterell translation, 1971 edition. The lectures were recorded in shorthand and not revised by Steiner. References below use the supplied capture’s page markers; another edition may paginate differently.

These are original teaching summaries and activities, not a reproduction of the book. The English parallel reading is available below; Portuguese explanations are original course text. Cultural generalizations, Atlantis, cosmic physiology and political judgments are studied as Steiner’s claims in context. The course does not treat an evocative story as independent evidence for them.
