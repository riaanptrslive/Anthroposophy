Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

Section 6.1 revisits four images: mechanistic, biological or nativist, psychological or empiricist, and personalistic. The first foregrounds mechanisms; the second inherited constitution; the third learning and environment; the fourth individuality, meaning and self-direction. This is Lievegoed’s classification.

Every standpoint makes some questions easier to ask. A limited method can be useful without becoming a complete picture of a person. Lievegoed criticizes turning partial explanations into exclusive accounts and explicitly acknowledges that his own standpoint shapes his book.

His discussion of Rogers emphasizes empathy and respect that support a person’s own development. Maslow and Allport contribute a concern with becoming, creativity and more than mere adjustment. Lievegoed’s spiritual personalism adds its own metaphysical commitments to that concern.

An explanatory model changes what counts as a question

A difficulty can be approached through bodily mechanisms, inherited disposition, learned patterns or questions of meaning and choice. Lievegoed’s images of the human being bring these emphases into view. A method may be useful for a particular question without explaining the whole person. The philosophical mistake he targets is turning one useful perspective into an exclusive account that makes other questions disappear.

Use a fictional person who repeatedly avoids a demanding task. One account asks about conditions and learned responses; another asks about the aim the person fears or values; another asks about bodily limitations. These questions need not have identical answers. Comparing Freud, Adler, Jung, Frankl or Assagioli in this chapter means understanding the explanatory emphasis Lievegoed attributes to each. It does not mean selecting a therapy for that fictional person. Separate the book’s philosophical comparison from evidence about treatment effectiveness and from the needs of any actual individual.

A passage to read closely

The reader has a right to know what those other possible standpoints are and which fields of knowledge can be explored from each one.

Bernard Lievegoed · Phases · Book chapter 6.1 · PDF capture page 91.

Read the wording

The reader’s “right to know” calls for making assumptions visible, including those of this book.

A worked example

A learner misses a deadline. One account considers fatigue, another work conditions, another habits, and another whether the task has meaning for the learner.

Different questions can complement each other. None is established merely by adopting the vocabulary of a preferred model.

Three key takeaways

  • A model directs attention.
  • Partial explanations need not be exclusive.
  • Make the author’s standpoint visible too.
