Adolescence: identity, ideals and choice

Connect self-discovery with real opportunities to act and learn.

Understand → read closely → apply → revise

Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

Lievegoed presents adolescence through three questions: identity, desire and capability. Ideals can be intense while judgment remains provisional. The young person needs room to test a view of the world and discover how an intention survives contact with reality.

His discussion of Schiller’s play impulse connects freedom with form and material conditions. Play offers a way of participating imaginatively rather than merely submitting or rebelling. Freedom grows through a meaningful relation to limits and responsibility.

Education should broaden horizons while providing concrete tasks. Prematurely narrowing a person to repetitive work can restrict exploration; prolonged dependence can also postpone responsibility. The chapter values the phase itself, even as it prepares adult choices.

A passage to read closely

The crucial questions are therefore: Who am I? What do I want? What am I capable of?

Bernard Lievegoed · Phases · Book chapter 2.2 · PDF capture page 32.

Read the wording

The three questions belong together. Wanting something and being able to do it are related, but not identical.

A worked example

A student wants to help the environment and tries a supervised community garden project. She discovers that she enjoys organizing volunteers more than designing equipment.

A concrete experience refines the ideal without making one early choice a permanent identity.

Three key takeaways

  • Ask about identity, intention and capability.
  • Give ideals opportunities to meet experience.
  • Early choices can remain revisable.
