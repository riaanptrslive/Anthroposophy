Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

The second half of Chapter 1 compares images of the human being and several ways of dividing life. The mechanistic, biological, psychological and personalistic images each emphasize different explanations. The chosen image affects which aspects of a biography become visible.

Lievegoed places Steiner alongside thinkers including Bühler, Rümke, Guardini and Moers. Guardini’s value-centre gives each phase its own significance: childhood is not simply an incomplete adulthood. Moers’s vital-mental, objective and spiritual strivings help describe changing dominant concerns.

The seven-year rhythms provide approximate landmarks. Lievegoed explicitly says they are not fixed calendar dates, although he sometimes treats departures from his pattern too normatively. Read for useful questions about transitions and test the fit against the particular life.

Use a phase to generate questions, then check the fit

A developmental map directs attention toward a possible pattern. It becomes misleading when the pattern is used as the evidence for itself: “this must be a crisis because the person has reached this age.” Lievegoed qualifies his phase ages, so a careful reader begins with what is actually changing and only then asks whether the map illuminates it.

Compare two people of the same age, one beginning a new occupation and one caring for a family member. Their outward roles do not tell us which values they are reconsidering or which capacities they are developing. Ask about the transition, the available choices and the meaning each gives it. Then identify both a fit and a mismatch with the proposed phase. The value-centre of a period means that it has its own concerns and significance; it need not be justified solely as preparation for the next stage.

A passage to read closely

Instead, they are milestones, averages, ideal distances around which individual development moves.

Bernard Lievegoed · Phases · Book chapter 1.4–1.5 · PDF capture page 25.

Read the wording

“Around which” leaves room for variation. Read this qualification alongside the more definite age statements later in the book.

A worked example

Two people begin new studies at 29 and 54. A rigid chart calls one late; a biographical reading asks what this learning means for each.

Age supplies context. It cannot establish the person’s motives, opportunities or level of maturity on its own.

Three key takeaways

  • Each phase has value in itself.
  • Age ranges organize inquiry.
  • The biography can challenge the model.

Does a missed age landmark prove immaturity?

Show an explained answer

No. An approximate model cannot diagnose a person.
