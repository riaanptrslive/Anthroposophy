Relationships: meeting the other person

Explain projection, mutual freedom and the renewal of shared life.

Understand → read closely → apply → revise

Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

Chapter 3, Male and Female Development—Marriage, applies the phase model to relationships. Lievegoed begins with the spiritual equivalence of individualities but interprets psychological differences through a gender binary. His discussion draws on Jung’s anima, animus and shadow: images within us can shape what we see in a partner.

Projection occurs when our expectations obscure the actual person. Early idealization can later turn into a caricature of everything wrong with the relationship. The author asks whether each partner can recognize their own contribution to the conflict and meet the other anew. He explicitly calls his central marriage narrative idealized.

The constructive themes are mutual freedom, shared joys and sorrows, and a culture of belonging. Stories, celebrations and preparation for shared occasions connect generations. The chapter also acknowledges development outside marriage. These themes can be explored in varied households and relationships.

Recognizing projection changes the question you ask

Projection means that an image or expectation contributed by oneself is experienced as though it were simply a property of the other person. In a relationship, someone may first seem to embody every hoped-for quality and later every disappointment. Lievegoed asks whether the actual individual can become visible through these changing images. That requires more than replacing a flattering label with an unflattering one.

Take a disagreement about a shared task. Separate what was said or done, the meaning each person attributed to it and what each expected beforehand. This can reveal an assumption without deciding that all conflict is imagined or that responsibility is equal in every situation. Meeting the other person includes respecting boundaries and concrete conduct. The book’s gendered categories belong to its historical framework; the useful reading task is to examine how expectations affect understanding without making gender determine an individual’s inner life.

A passage to read closely

A high level of tolerance is necessary here in order to permit one’s partner the freedom which one takes for granted for oneself.

Bernard Lievegoed · Phases · Book chapter 3 · PDF capture page 73.

Read the wording

The test is reciprocal: does the freedom I claim extend to the other person? Agreement on every belief is not required.

A worked example

One partner wants time for a new interest. The other initially treats this as rejection, then asks what the interest means and discusses shared commitments.

Checking the interpretation makes space for individuality. Mutual responsibility can be discussed without demanding identical interests.

Three key takeaways

  • Distinguish the person from your image of them.
  • Freedom should be reciprocal.
  • Shared culture can support belonging.

Keep the context clear
