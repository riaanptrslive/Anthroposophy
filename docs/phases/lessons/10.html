After 56: letting go and remaining creative

Connect changing roles with values that can take new forms.

Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

The later phase asks whether values have become lived convictions. Roles, authority and physical capacities change; what remains meaningful when an established way of expressing oneself is no longer available? Lievegoed treats this as another developmental question.

Letting go can include preparing a successor, revising expectations of retirement and acknowledging unfinished work. A recurring theme may find a smaller or different expression. The person’s significance need not depend on maintaining an earlier level of output.

The chapter gives examples of late creativity and proposes cultural life that connects older people with a living community. The possibility matters more than making exceptional artists a standard everyone must meet. Receiving care and participating in relationships also belong to a life.

Experience becomes a contribution through its use

Years of activity can produce knowledge, but the passage of time alone does not make that knowledge useful to another person. Mentoring requires selecting what matters, explaining why it matters and leaving the learner room to judge. Lievegoed’s contrast between protecting status and giving experience a future concerns this change of relationship to one’s own achievements.

Imagine an experienced craftsperson preparing a successor. Taking over every difficult task preserves immediate quality but prevents the successor from learning. Giving a task, explaining its purpose and discussing the result can turn personal skill into another person’s growing capacity. Letting go is active work here, not indifference. Later contribution can also take quieter forms, including receiving help and sustaining relationships. The book’s examples of exceptional creativity illustrate possibilities; they do not establish a standard by which to judge someone whose circumstances or capacities differ.

Reading connection: Phases · Chapter 2 · later adulthood.

A passage to read closely

It is a well-known fact that creativity at an advanced age is not impossible.

Bernard Lievegoed · Phases · Book chapter 2.8 · PDF capture page 60.

Read the wording

“Not impossible” opens a possibility. It does not promise renewed productivity or make creativity a duty.

A worked example

A retired musician cannot maintain a concert schedule. She enjoys discussing recordings with a friend and arranging one simple piece.

The form changes while a valued relationship to music continues. Her worth would remain even if she chose rest instead.

Three key takeaways

  • A value can outlive a particular role.
  • Later creativity is possible in many forms.
  • Contribution does not determine human worth.
