In a learning group

A familiar activity no longer engages a learner. Ask what has changed in their interests, relationships and understanding.

Understand the idea

Koepke reads the ninth-year transition as a change in the child’s relation to self and world. Lievegoed’s account of adolescence introduces identity, intention and capability. These later authors develop anthroposophical interpretations; their contexts and age ranges differ.

A developmental map can suggest a question while leaving the actual encounter open. Listen to what the child says, examine circumstances and allow another explanation. An age does not by itself establish what an individual needs.

Apply the chapter’s concepts

A child wants more privacy and also reassurance. These wishes can coexist without one simple label explaining them.

Which of the concepts above helps explain this situation? Keep the illustration distinct from the author’s wider claims.

Work with the idea

Describe a fictional child’s changing request. Write one developmental interpretation and one alternative. List what you would ask before choosing a response.

Check your understanding

What is missing if an adult explains the wish for privacy only by the child’s age?

Compare with explained feedback

The child’s account, recent circumstances and the particular relationship remain unknown. A phase model may prompt attention, but the response should fit what is learned about this child.

When the situation changes

The child explains that the group laughs at unfinished work. What does this change in the interpretation and support?

Return to the request after listening. Name a part of the explanation you retained and a part you revised.
