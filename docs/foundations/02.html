Observation, interpretation and knowledge

Distinguish what you encounter from the explanation you form.

Understand the relationship

An observation describes what you notice. An interpretation connects what you notice with an explanation. Knowledge requires examining that connection. If a plant leans toward a window, its position is observable; an account of how light affects its growth introduces an explanatory relationship. You should be able to say what supports that relationship and what might require revision.

Thinking is already involved when we distinguish, compare and connect. The aim is not to eliminate thinking in favour of supposedly pure observation, but to become conscious of what thinking contributes. “It happened after this” and “it happened because of this” are different statements.

Steiner’s opening account in Theosophy distinguishes sensory encounter, personal response and understanding. We will develop those terms in the next lesson. For now, separate the event, its significance for you and your account of it. A single sentence may contain all three.

Use this distinction throughout the course. A spiritual claim also needs to be understood accurately: what is asserted, how the author arrives at it, and what kind of access to it is claimed? Neither agreement nor dismissal replaces understanding the argument.

An observation becomes knowledge through a relation

Suppose two leaves on a plant turn toward the window. Recording their position is an observation. Saying they changed because of light introduces an explanation. To examine it, one must consider the earlier position, the interval and other conditions, rather than merely repeat the observed result. Thinking contributes the proposed relation, and further observation can help determine whether that relation fits.

This distinction does not divide life into a pure sensory world and an unrelated world of ideas. It identifies different contributions within knowing. A concept helps us ask what to notice; what we notice can require us to revise the concept’s application. In spiritual-scientific texts, an additional claim may concern a mode of perception beyond ordinary senses. Identify that step explicitly so that a familiar example of ordinary inquiry is not mistaken for verification of the further claim.
