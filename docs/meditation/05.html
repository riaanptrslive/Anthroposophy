The symbol gathers a thought and feeling sequence

The Rose Cross is not introduced merely as a striking image. Its preparation compares plant life and human life, including the relation between desire, suffering and the possibility of transformation. The final image gathers meanings developed through that preparation. Without the sequence, the symbol becomes an unexplained object whose supposed effect is simply asserted.

Explain the preparatory contrasts in your own words before working with the image. Then distinguish thinking through the meaning from dwelling with the completed symbol. The point is not to force an unusual visual experience or to treat artistic vividness as evidence of spiritual perception. The learner should be able to say what the image gathers and why the preparation gives it that meaning.

Rudolf Steiner · An Outline of Esoteric Science · GA 13 · The Rose Cross · § 10

It is good, outside the time devoted to meditation itself, frequently to repeat the construction of the image through thoughts and feelings of the kind described above, so that the feeling does not fade.

Compare with the German passage

Jedoch ist es gut, wenn man sich außer der Zeit, welche man der eigentlichen Versenkung widmet, öfters durch Gedanken und Gefühle der oben geschilderten Art den Aufbau des Bildes wiederholt, damit die Empfindung nicht verblasse.

What this passage means

This is Steiner’s distinction between preparing the Rose Cross and meditating with it. Preparation builds the meaning through plant, human passions and transformation; meditation holds the formed image with the feeling that preparation awakened.

The focus of this lesson

Understand and construct the symbolic meditation through its preparatory thoughts and feelings.

Why this comes here

After working with words, examine how Steiner uses an image. Its meaning must be built; displaying a symbol alone does not teach the practice.

What the source is teaching

Steiner first contrasts plant growth with human freedom, desires and the possibility of transforming impulses. He then develops the rose as an image of purified desire and the black cross as an image of what has been overcome. The seven red roses at the crossing express the transformation. These are symbolic relations in his account, not botanical claims about sap and blood.

The crucial direction is to build the image through thoughts accompanied by feeling before meditating on it. During the meditation, the explanatory argument recedes while the image and the feeling prepared by it remain. At other times the preparation can be renewed. This distinction makes the exercise more than either looking at a picture or continuing an intellectual essay.

How to work with it

Historical directions are identified above. The guided steps below are our teaching arrangement of the selected practice.

  1. Read and reconstruct the contrast: plant growth; human capacities and impulses; the possibility of conscious transformation.
  2. Form the image of a black cross with seven radiant red roses in a circle at the intersection. Explain to yourself what each element signifies in the source.
  3. If choosing this practice, dwell briefly with the internally formed image and its prepared meaning, allowing the explanatory sequence to rest. This is the source method retold; our brief introduction is not its complete training programme.
  4. Afterward, distinguish the image you deliberately formed from something perceived externally. Explain why preparation changes its significance. If visualization is difficult, a written account demonstrates understanding; it is not an identical experience of image meditation.

Check the connection

Why does the source insist on building the image before meditating?

Read an explanation

The preparatory thoughts and feelings give the symbol its inward significance. A ready-made decoration lacks that developed relationship. During meditation the symbol carries the prepared feeling rather than requiring the whole argument to be repeated.
