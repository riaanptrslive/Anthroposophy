Spiritual Beings I: learning to attend to nature

Trace Steiner’s movement from sensory impressions to an inner, moral response to nature.

Understand → read closely → apply → revise

Understand the chapter

The key ideas, explained

Steiner begins with a distinction between the physical field of action and spiritual impulses expressed within it. A moral action still happens in the physical world. He then argues that studying spiritual science changes the person who studies: interests, attention and feeling develop. Knowledge here means a transformation in how someone encounters the world, as well as learning concepts.

His examples follow a sequence. One attends to the blue sky, the green of plants, white snow or the relation of a tone to its octave. The sensory impression is then allowed to recede, leaving a distinctive inner response: devotion, an experience of creative thinking, or harmony between desire and reason. These examples explain what he means by educating feeling. He criticizes merely repeating loving sentiments as if that alone were spiritual understanding.

Behind physical nature he describes an etheric or life world. Unlike the connected etheric body he attributes to an individual human being, nature’s etheric world contains a multiplicity of beings. He approaches them through differences of form and activity: bounded forms, continual transformation and other modes of manifestation. These are descriptions of claimed supersensible perception. The next lecture develops the four elemental domains and their coordination.

A passage to read closely

Spiritual science does not merely signify the acquisition of knowledge; it signifies most preeminently an education, a self-education of our souls.

Rudolf Steiner · What Is Biodynamics? · Book chapter 1 · PDF capture page 36.

Read the wording

The semicolon connects two meanings of study: acquiring something and becoming capable of something. Read the nearby discussion of changing interests to make “self-education” concrete.

A worked example

Two students describe the same leaf. One records its shape and colour; the other records those features and then notices a feeling of freshness.

The second account adds an inner response without erasing observation. Neither account alone proves an elemental being. This distinction lets us understand the proposed transition without pretending that a feeling and a public observation are identical.

Three key takeaways

  • Attention itself is part of the subject.
  • Distinguish a sensory impression from the response it awakens.
  • Nature’s etheric realm is presented as a multiplicity.
