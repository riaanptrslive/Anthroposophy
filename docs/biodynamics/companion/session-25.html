Source commentary · PDF capture 96

Means's six-preparation overview

Inspect this page’s inventory

Means summarizes the six compost preparations through substances and functions. Steiner then repeats that ordinary manuring continues and that life must remain within the living realm. The mound/bark analogy reconnects this chapter to Agriculture I: soil around roots is conceived as an extension of vegetative life. This continuity explains why the next preparations are applied to manure rather than simply substituted for its bulk. Means's concise nutrient language should be read beside Steiner's stronger emphasis on forces, receptivity and organic processes. Otherwise the preparations can look like ordinary packets of mineral nutrients, which is precisely not how the lecture explains them.

Source commentary · PDF capture 97

Acknowledge legitimate modern achievements

Inspect this page’s inventory

Steiner explicitly asks that legitimate modern achievements be acknowledged and supplemented, rather than attacked indiscriminately. His reason for treating manure is that exporting farm products removes forces that must be restored. The dirty-room/flies analogy then argues that microorganisms indicate a condition instead of causing it: changing the flies would not remove the dirt. The analogy makes his causal priority vivid, but does not establish that bacteria are merely indicators in manure. Soil organisms actively transform organic matter and cycle nutrients in the scientific account, E4. The page's methodological moderation and its specific scientific disagreement should both be taught; neither cancels the other.

Scientific comparison · Microbes and soil life

Soil organisms actively contribute to decomposition and nutrient cycling. This differs from treating microbes only as signs of an underlying condition. Acknowledging active microbes does not mean every commercial inoculant will be effective.

Source commentary · PDF capture 98

Positive proposals needed with criticism

Inspect this page’s inventory

Criticism, Steiner says, should be accompanied by positive proposals rather than only arouse opposition. His proposal requires organic materials capable of enlivening solid soil, in contrast to mineral additions acting mainly through water. He prioritizes the macrocosm over microscopic explanation. The nutrient discussion then divides substances whose availability depends on manuring from trace substances allegedly supplied through the heavens and rain. The claimed cosmic supply of lead, mercury and arsenic is not a recommendation to add them, nor does their possible presence establish nutritional benefit. This distinction prepares his next concern: soil must be made receptive, not merely loaded with substances. The spiritual explanation and nutrient chemistry remain separate accounts.

Source commentary · PDF capture 99

Exhaustion despite manure

Inspect this page’s inventory

Repeated farming can, Steiner argues, exhaust soil even when manure is used, because the ability to receive subtle influences has deteriorated. His answer is not simply more material but a changed capacity to take up what is offered. Kolisko's small-dose work is invoked as scientific support for homeopathic effects; this passage does not supply its methods, controls or results for evaluation. Horn sprays enhance ordinary manure, while the coming additions are intended to give manure its own vitality, consistency and retention of nitrogen. Students should distinguish an appeal to research from access to that research. Neither the smallness of a dose nor the name of a researcher settles efficacy.
