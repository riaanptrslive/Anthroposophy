Source commentary · PDF capture 93

Before-sowing preference and late trial

Inspect this page’s inventory

Steiner prefers manuring before sowing but acknowledges a late application whose outcome remains to be seen. He repeats a limited number of horn uses. Asked who can do the work, he attributes differences in plant care partly to a person's meditative relation to nitrogen and imaginations. The text then suggests seasonal concentration as a possible means of influencing parasites, especially during winter inwardness. These are claims of personal and spiritual causation; no controlled comparison is supplied. The social difficulty of discussing them does not validate them. Students should separate the observable possibility that different carers obtain different results from the additional explanation assigned to meditation.

Source commentary · PDF capture 94

Peasant philosophy and cultural loss

Inspect this page’s inventory

The seasonal qualification leads into Steiner's regret over unwritten peasant philosophy: dialect, customs and almanacs are treated as repositories of intimate environmental knowledge. The candy attached to an old almanac is a memorable cultural detail, not agricultural evidence. Most importantly, he rejects relying on private intuition alone when communicating quantities. Initial judgment should be translated into numerical tables that others can use. That instruction limits an overly romantic reading of his method. He calls for accommodation to others' standards of understanding, then begins to qualify lime use by soil type. Students can connect respectful communication, explicit measurement and local knowledge without assuming they independently confirm the preparations.

Source commentary · PDF capture 95

Compost turning and covering

Inspect this page’s inventory

The practical discussion finishes with soil-dependent lime amounts, compost turning followed by covering, and potash magnesia as the earlier-mentioned transitional material. Steiner unequivocally says horn manure does not replace ordinary manure. That statement should govern interpretations of tiny-dose treatment throughout the chapter. The notes identify black-earth regions, explain feldspar and orthoclase, attach numbers 500 and 501 to the sprays, distinguish Ritter remedies from biodynamics, and clarify the roughly eight-liter Swiss bucket. The notes also acknowledge country and climate variation in quantities. These editorial explanations turn local language into usable historical context; they do not remove the lecture's own provisional details.
