Source commentary · PDF capture 89

Strainer concession

Inspect this page’s inventory

Once the sprayer issue is clarified, Steiner accepts a strainer. He identifies a Swiss milk bucket but says weights and measures still need development. Horn reuse is tentative—three or four times is suggested without a completed test. On sourcing, an initial broad acceptance is qualified by freshness, local adaptation, hemispheric differences and time spent grazing locally. Cow age is also referred to experiment, with middle age provisionally preferred; horn size is illustrated from local cattle. The page therefore teaches how a general principle becomes a set of practical questions. It does not supply one settled specification independent of animal, place and experience.

Source commentary · PDF capture 90

Female cow versus bull/ox horns

Inspect this page’s inventory

The female cow is distinguished from bull and castrated cattle in the proposed horn effect. Sowing nearer winter is associated with reproduction, earlier sowing with nutrition. Sand application and rainfall are explicitly unresolved: rain might help establishment or disturb the subtle effect. The storage discussion favors minimal interference and criticizes excessive disinfection, but this is not contemporary hygiene advice. “Chaos” in the seed means a breakdown of previous organization that allows new formative activity, not random carelessness. Steiner says no extra intervention is needed to intensify it. These qualifications matter because they prevent the reader from treating every possible manipulation as beneficial merely because it sounds spiritual.

Source commentary · PDF capture 91

Optional silica and wheat/sainfoin comparison

Inspect this page’s inventory

Additional soil silica is possible but considered unnecessary here. The suggested wheat/sainfoin comparison raises a research question about differing seed responses; experimental plot size is left to practice. His statements about suppression or delay should remain as printed rather than be regularized into a modern test result. Storage rules distinguish the prepared material from the already diluted liquid: horns can remain buried or be protected with peat, whereas dilution should be close to use. Fine spraying is said not to lose the bound forces. Silica horns receive a different storage judgment. The differences follow his proposed material and seasonal polarity; they are not evidence that the forces have been measured.

Source commentary · PDF capture 92

Humus burial site

Inspect this page’s inventory

A humus-containing site is preferred for burial, without requiring each horn to be buried in its eventual treatment field. Steiner rejects a blanket ban on machinery, while preserving his special reservation about intimate stirring. He also opposes buying machinery merely for novelty. Excess concentration, he predicts, produces rank growth rather than the desired crop, including sprawling potato stems; more is not automatically better. Even fodder and spinach are not treated as justification for unlimited dosing. Cow manure remains preferred, while horse manure is proposed for investigation. This page combines practical flexibility with specific spiritual distinctions, making a simple “anti-machine” summary inaccurate.

Does this chapter ban farm machinery?

Read the explained answer

No. It accepts machinery as practically necessary and distinguishes particular intimate processes, especially stirring, from a blanket prohibition. It also criticizes novelty-driven purchasing.
