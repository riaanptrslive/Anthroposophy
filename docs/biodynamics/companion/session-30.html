Source commentary · PDF capture 114

Highlighted Means near/distant planet distinction

Inspect this page’s inventory

Your highlight belongs to Means's introduction. It distinguishes Mercury, Venus and Moon influences entering through soil and supporting reproduction from Jupiter, Mars and Saturn influences supporting fruiting. These are two ideas, not simply a list of six heavenly bodies. “Fruiting” here needs the next pages: edible expansion and propagation into the next generation are being contrasted, although ordinary language can associate both with fruit. Means then confidently summarizes pest and disease control. Steiner's actual opening is narrower: he will offer concrete examples as starting points for experiments. The difference in tone is worth retaining. Pages 115–117 explain the planetary distinctions behind the highlight; they do not establish them as findings of astronomy or plant physiology.

Source commentary · PDF capture 115

Weed as human relational category

Inspect this page’s inventory

A weed has no intrinsic lesser right to exist in nature; it is unwanted relative to a particular human purpose and place. That relational definition is the starting point for asking how to discourage a plant locally. Steiner separates near-planet influences acting indirectly through Earth and lime from distant-planet influences mediated by silica and the surroundings. Even silica in the soil can, in his model, carry the latter group, so physical location and spiritual origin are not identical. Phylloxera serves as a historical example of agricultural difficulty that he attributes to lost cosmic understanding. The example motivates his proposal but does not demonstrate that only the proposed method could solve the problem.

Source commentary · PDF capture 116

Seed succession versus edible expansion

Inspect this page’s inventory

Near-planet activity is assigned to the sequence of generations ending in seed; distant-planet activity to expansion such as edible fruit flesh. Thus a peach can illustrate two different functions within one plant. Steiner treats moonlight as reflected sunlight altered by lunar forces and places that activity within an earlier Moon–Earth unity. Reproduction is described as growth intensified until a separate organism emerges. This conceptual continuity explains why he thinks reducing reproductive forces could restrain weeds without merely destroying all growth. The cosmic history and planetary assignments are spiritual assertions. They are not consequences established by the observable fact that the Moon reflects sunlight.

Source commentary · PDF capture 117

Moon as cosmic reflector

Inspect this page’s inventory

The Moon is said to reflect the wider cosmos as well as sunlight, and Steiner acknowledges that this cannot then be physically demonstrated. Full-moon activity is privileged, but he adds a qualification: effects can persist or wait in the soil, so an untimely human action does not necessarily fail immediately. Wet conditions are said to strengthen the lunar action, benefiting weeds as well as crops. The proposed control strategy is to change soil receptivity to that influence rather than switch off the Moon. These premises explain the logic of the coming ash method. The allowance for delayed effects also complicates testing, because the expected timing must be specified rather than adjusted after any outcome.
