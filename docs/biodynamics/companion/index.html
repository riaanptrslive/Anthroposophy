What “biodynamics” means in this book

Courtney presents a spiritual approach to farming organized around the farm as a living individuality, relationships with the wider cosmos, and specially made preparations. The selected Steiner lectures supply a spiritual account of perception and nature, then agricultural proposals. Understanding the book means explaining those connections while distinguishing its assertions from independently established findings.

The course assesses understanding, attribution and reasoned judgment. Agreement with its spiritual claims is not required. It is a reading course, with observation and paper-based exercises, rather than instructions to carry out the historical treatments.

A method for close reading

  1. Read the assigned captures in your copy, including the chapter introduction and notes.
  2. Identify who is speaking and reconstruct the claim, terms, reasons and qualifications.
  3. Read the page commentary. Distinguish an observation, an analogy, an asserted premise and a demonstrated result.
  4. Answer the lesson question before opening the explanation. Revise your answer with page references.

All examples labeled “Teaching example” are original instructor examples. They illustrate reasoning; they are not reported events from the book. Use your own notebook. These new companion pages do not save responses.

Plan your study

Allow roughly 45–75 minutes per lesson, including reading and written response; dense sections may take longer. One or two lessons per meeting gives an 18–36 meeting route. Lessons 00 and 35 provide orientation and final assessment. The introduction receives seven lessons because its arguments cannot be reduced to a preface summary.

This expansion is in English. The existing shorter course is available in both languages. Read the explicit coverage limits before using the companion as a source-completeness claim.

Final portfolio

  1. Write a 500–800 word definition of biodynamics as presented in this book, distinguishing Courtney, Means and Steiner.
  2. Explain each of the eleven highlighted ideas, including its surrounding argument and page reference.
  3. Choose one argument from every numbered chapter. State its premises, example or reason, conclusion and any unresolved step.
  4. Compare two source claims with the linked scientific explanations. Explain the actual disagreement.
  5. Create a garden or farm portrait separating your observations from the book’s interpretations. Design a hypothetical comparison with specified outcomes and alternative explanations.
  6. List remaining uncertainties and identify the additional sources needed to investigate them.

Assessment: source accuracy 30%; reasoning and terms 30%; qualifications and evidence distinctions 25%; clear teaching examples and questions 15%. Full credit is available without accepting a spiritual claim. A name or passing mention does not count as an explanation.
