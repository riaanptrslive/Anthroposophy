00 — How to study this book

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: First-edition and 1918 prefaces; Basis markers 1–4.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Begin with two questions: how do I know, and what leads me to act?

Detailed teaching

A friend says the reading group starts at ten. You remember nine. Rather than choose the most confident person, you check the current announcement and ask whether the time changed. Your memory, your friend’s statement and the announcement are different things to examine.

Now ask why you offer to help set up the meeting. Is it habit, pressure, a wish for approval, or a reason you have considered and adopted? Finding the meeting time and understanding your motive are different inquiries. They introduce the book’s connected questions about knowing and free action.

Study in two short stages if helpful. First practice reading actively: state a question, explain the author’s reasoning in your own words, and return to the passage to check. Then read the 1918 preface’s account of the two investigations. Do not assume the conclusions before following the arguments.

These lessons adapt the reviewed book material and the explanatory questions developed in Brian’s lectures. The student examples are original teaching material. Steiner’s argument, a lecturer’s interpretation and your own judgment are distinguishable. Understanding an argument does not require agreeing with it.

Activity

Write one question about knowing and one about acting. Choose a short passage from a preface, restate its claim, and identify one reason the author gives. Record what remains unclear.

Questions and explained answers

Does confidence settle the meeting time?

No. Confidence is something about the speaker; the time needs relevant evidence.

Can you understand an argument and still question it?

Yes. Explain its reasoning fairly, then assess whether that reasoning supports its conclusion.

Does being able to help establish freedom?

No. Practical ability and the source of a motive are different questions.

Teaching conclusion

Read, restate, examine the reasons, and return to the text.

01 — Is choosing enough?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: GA 4, Chapter 1; Basis 6–11.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

distinguish having options, carrying out a choice and understanding how a motive arises.

Detailed teaching

You receive an invitation to a study group. Tuesday and Saturday are available. You choose Tuesday. That tells us which option you selected, but not why. Perhaps you usually attend on Tuesdays, want someone to approve of you, or have examined what you hope to learn. Several influences may operate together.

Now suppose Tuesday's meeting is cancelled. You cannot carry out your plan. That is a practical obstacle; it does not explain the origin of the decision. Conversely, an open door and a free evening do not establish that your motive is free.

In the stone thought experiment quoted by Steiner, a moving stone is imagined to be aware of its striving while ignorant of the impulse that started its motion. It would mistake awareness for authorship. Steiner asks whether this comparison does justice to actions whose reasons a person can investigate and understand. The analogy raises a problem; it does not settle the whole question about human action.

Even “I acted rationally” needs examination. How did that reason become yours? Recognizing a wish for approval is different from understanding an ethical reason. Chapter 1 leaves us with the need to study thinking and knowing. It also refuses to reduce human action to cold calculation: understanding another person's situation can awaken care.

Activity

choose a small decision. Write what you did, what options and obstacles existed, and what you believe moved you. Separate what you know from what you infer. Name a possible influence you have not fully examined. You need not disclose personal material to the group.

Questions and explained answers

Does identifying a motive prove freedom?

No. It distinguishes awareness from blind impulse, but leaves questions about the motive's origin and the kind of understanding involved. The chapter prepares that inquiry.

What does the stone illustrate?

Awareness of striving without knowledge of its cause. Extending the analogy to every human action requires an argument.

Does thinking exclude feeling?

No. A person's understanding can participate in compassion or love; this does not make every interpretation of another person correct.

Teaching conclusion

Having options, executing an action and understanding its motive are different questions.

02 — Why do we seek explanations?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: GA 4, Chapter 2; Basis 12–16.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Why do we seek explanations?

Detailed teaching

You look at a branch. It is still. Later it moves. You have noticed a difference, but you may still want to know why. Perhaps wind moved it; perhaps someone touched it. Naming a possibility does not settle the question. You look for further evidence and may have to leave the cause uncertain.

Steiner begins Chapter 2 with this desire to understand what experience gives us. We encounter a world that does not immediately answer all our questions. We experience ourselves as the ones asking and the world as what we seek to understand. Yet we also belong to that world. This is the tension the chapter explores.

Consider a drawing with “I” on one side and “World” on the other. It can represent the distinction between the questioner and what is questioned. It does not prove that they are two realities with nothing in common. Drawing a bridge or placing both labels inside one circle does not explain their relation either.

Steiner calls attention to attempts at separation and unity. A dualist account treats the two sides as fundamentally distinct and must explain their connection. A monist account seeks one underlying reality and must explain the differences we experience. In this chapter he examines approaches that give priority to matter, to spirit, or to their union in the simplest entity. His objection is that an answer may name or relocate the problem without explaining it. These are his arguments to examine, not conclusions you must accept to pass the lesson.

The proposed next step is to investigate our own participation in knowing. We are part of nature, so Steiner asks what in us can disclose our connection with it. Brian points ahead to thinking. The chapter itself leaves a task for the inquiry that follows. Its closing paragraph also warns against treating these initial everyday descriptions as completed scientific results or fully settled technical definitions.

Activity

choose an ordinary event, such as a light going out. Separate what you observed, the question it raised, two possible explanations and one thing you could check without dismantling anything. Then describe your part in forming the question. An unanswered question is an acceptable outcome.

Questions and explained answers

Is an explanation just another description?

A description reports what was noticed; an explanation seeks the relation or conditions that account for it. A proposed explanation still needs support.

Does distinguishing self and world establish absolute dualism?

No. It describes an experienced contrast; a claim that they are fundamentally separate needs further argument. Declaring unity also leaves a relation to explain.

What has the chapter established at this stage?

It describes the problem and proposes a direction for investigation. Steiner explicitly says these opening descriptions are not yet scientific results, and his terms are not yet used with full technical precision.

Teaching conclusion

wanting to understand takes us from noticing the world to examining both our explanation and our part in knowing.

03 — What do we add by thinking?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: GA 4, Chapter 3; Basis 17–19.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

What do we add by thinking?

Detailed teaching

Imagine watching two billiard balls. One approaches the other; contact occurs; their movements change. You can describe the sequence. To explain it, you connect ideas such as motion, impact and the conditions of this particular encounter. Your thinking adds a conceptual process to your experience of the event; it does not create the balls or push them.

An explanation can support a prediction when its concepts and assumptions fit the case. Guessing what happens behind a screen is not the same as observing it. A prediction must still be compared with what occurs. This distinction continues Chapter 2's movement from a question to an examined explanation.

Steiner uses observation broadly: feelings, dreams and memories can also become things we attend to. Noticing a dream of a horse does not establish that a horse is in the room. Thinking about a horse does not produce one either. The experience, the judgment about it and the object claimed by that judgment should be distinguished.

The chapter begins with thinking as activity that appears to be ours. Whether this proves independence is left open at this point. For now, ask what changes when you actively work out a relation rather than only receive an impression or repeat an answer.

Activity

use a fictional case: a cup stood on a table; a moment later it was on the floor. List the stated observations, two possible explanations and what further information would distinguish them. Label the explanation as an inference, not something already seen.

Questions and explained answers

Does thinking change the observed event?

the example concerns a change in understanding, not a thought pushing the object.

Does having a vivid memory establish its accuracy?

no; the occurrence of the memory and its accuracy are different questions.

Has the opening proved freedom?

no; Steiner postpones the question of the activity's independence.

Teaching conclusion

thinking seeks connections; knowing an explanation requires more than simply having one.

04 — Thinking: make a connection, then examine it

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 3 in full, including the 1918 addition; Basis 17–28.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Distinguish an observed event, an explanation and reflection on your own thinking.

Detailed teaching

Calculate ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2. Keep your steps. Before reading on, distinguish the result from the activity by which you reached it.

When two billiard balls meet, you can observe a sequence of movements. An explanation connects motion, contact and the conditions of the encounter. Thinking contributes an understood relationship; it does not push the balls. A prediction based on that relationship still needs comparison with what happens.

In the arithmetic example, the steps are 3 × 3 = 9, 9 + 1 = 10, and 10 ÷ 2 = 5. Now make those connections your object of attention. Remembering “five” is different from reconstructing why it follows. Applying division only to the 1 changes the expression, not merely the wording of the answer.

Steiner distinguishes thinking about a problem from subsequently thinking about that activity. You may have been aware of your reasons while solving it; the review makes them the subject of a fresh investigation. He gives thinking a special place because we participate in producing its connections and examine them through thinking itself. This is his proposed starting point, not a guarantee that every explanation is correct.

Observation here also includes inward experiences. A dream can be noticed without establishing its imagined object as physically present. Similarly, confidence can be observed without becoming a justification. The 1918 addition distinguishes active thinking from passing thought-images and relates willing to that activity. It does not require a blank mind. The next chapter asks how this activity relates to what we perceive.

Activity

write a three-step argument about an ordinary timetable, then cover it and reconstruct it. Compare the reconstruction with the original. Mark a missing assumption or a step that needs checking. If you only recalled the conclusion, work through the steps again.

Questions and explained answers

Is the answer identical to the activity?

no; the answer is a result, while the activity involves relating operations or reasons.

Does reflection require leaving the body?

no; this exercise changes the focus of attention.

Does confidence establish a correct judgment or a theory of the brain?

no; examine the reasons, the application and any additional claims separately.

Teaching conclusion

first think through something, then examine its connections; keep understanding distinct from certainty alone.

05 — Perception, representation and an argument to test

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 4 in full; Basis 29–40.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Distinguish what appears, what is remembered, and what an argument about perception establishes.

Detailed teaching

Imagine a circular card facing you, then tilted. The outline looks narrower. Has the card changed shape, or has your view changed? Put it away and describe what remains in memory.

A percept is the content encountered through observation, not the act of perceiving. In this book, inward feeling or a memory can also become an object of attention. A remembered experience is present now as memory; that does not guarantee its accuracy. The object, its appearance, the concept of a circle and a retained representation answer different questions.

Wilson’s “mental picture” and Amrine’s “representation” translate Vorstellung in corresponding discussions. The term does not require a vivid inner photograph. Keep the surrounding definition in view. The translation note helps you read two editions of the same argument without inventing two different mental faculties.

The chapter examines a further inference: perception depends on our organization, therefore we know only representations. Steiner questions the proof. It first treats eyes, nerves and brain as objects able to explain experience, then calls all perceived objects representations. What warrants the special status of the organism used as the foundation? This is a consistency objection, not permission to ignore bodily processes.

A faulty proof does not automatically make its conclusion false. Label the position being examined, Steiner’s reply, and the question still open. His account of consciousness connects observation and conceptual activity and asks how subject and object are distinguished within knowing. Actual learners need not strip away every concept to imitate an impossible blank observer. Chapter 5 develops the positive account of knowing.

Activity

Compare the card in two positions and then recall it out of sight. Label appearance, concept and representation. Reconstruct the “only representations” argument in three steps, explain Steiner’s objection, and name a conclusion that objection alone cannot prove.

Questions and explained answers

Is a percept the act of perceiving?

no; in this usage it is the encountered content.

Does a changing appearance prove the object imaginary?

no; it shows that the appearance depends partly on the encounter's conditions.

Does a weakness in a proof establish the opposite conclusion?

No. Explain the inconsistency, then distinguish rejecting a proof from disproving its conclusion.

Teaching conclusion

distinguish what appears, how you understand it and what remains in memory.

06 — Does perception give us only representations?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: GA 4, Chapter 4; Basis 35–40.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Does perception give us only representations?

Detailed teaching

Chapter 4 examines an argument that can sound persuasive: perception depends on our organization; therefore all we directly know is our own representations. Notice that the conclusion says more than the starting observation. Dependence on a perceiver and knowledge restricted to representations are not the same statement.

In the account Steiner examines, an external object affects sense organs, processes occur in the organism, and an experience results. The conclusion then treats the apparent external object as a representation. Steiner asks: what status did the argument give to the eyes, nerves and brain used to explain this result? They too are known through perception. If the argument doubts all perceived objects, it needs to explain why these particular ones were allowed to function as an unquestioned foundation.

This is his consistency objection to that route of proof. It is not a reason to ignore bodily processes. Nor does a defect in an argument automatically show its conclusion false. We must distinguish a claim, the reasons offered for it and the objection to those reasons.

Use three labels while reading: “position being examined,” “Steiner's response” and “question still open.” The quotation from Volkelt's book on Kant is not a direct quotation from Kant. The accounts discussed should not be collapsed into the slogan that everything disappears when nobody looks. The chapter ends by calling for another approach to the relation between percept and representation.

Activity

write the proposed argument in three steps. Underline the assumption about the organism. Explain Steiner's objection, then state one conclusion that the objection does not establish on its own.

Questions and explained answers

What does the opening viewpoint example establish?

dependence of appearance on conditions, not the complete unreality of an object.

What is the consistency question?

whether the same standard is applied to the perceived organism and other perceived objects.

If a proof fails, must its conclusion be false?

no; another argument might support it, and the opposing position also requires grounds.

Teaching conclusion

examine how a conclusion is supported before accepting either it or its opposite.

07 — Knowing: percept and concept belong together

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 5 in full, including its 1918 addition; Basis 41–53.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Reconstruct the account of knowing and distinguish shared meaning from identical personal experience.

Detailed teaching

Imagine three dated views of one flower: bud, opening flower and blossom. What lets you understand them as a development rather than three disconnected appearances?

Steiner argues that the percept is one side of reality and the fitting concept the other. We grasp the relationship through our activity, but he holds that its content belongs to the phenomenon. Thinking does not make the petals grow. Knowing a name does not yet explain a process, and a predicted next stage may fail when conditions change.

Consider two differently drawn triangles. The drawings and individual encounters differ; both students can nevertheless understand the same three-sided relationship. This illustrates shared conceptual content. It does not prove that everyone always means the same thing or settle Steiner’s wider claim of a universal conceptual whole. Ask each student to explain the connection.

In this discussion, intuition names the inward apprehension of thought content. It differs from a hunch, confidence or enthusiasm. When a drawing is removed, a representation of that encounter may remain; Chapter 6 examines its relation to the concept more closely. A memory can itself become an object of observation without being the original paper seen again.

The chapter continues the criticism of a proof that confines knowledge to mental pictures. Its positive alternative locates thinking within knowing reality. The 1918 addition asks the reader to examine assumptions about representation and the distinctive role of thinking. Keep two questions separate: can you reconstruct the proposed connection, and do its reasons justify the conclusion?

Activity

Map the flower as observed stages, conceptual relationship and untested expectation. Then use two triangles to explain how shared meaning differs from personal experience. Reconstruct the chapter’s proposal in three steps and identify one assumption you would question.

Questions and explained answers

Does a name explain development?

No; explaining requires connecting the stages and conditions.

Does thinking physically open the flower in this example?

No; it supplies a conceptual connection in the account of knowing.

Does shared conceptual content mean identical personal experience?

No. The triangle example distinguishes a common relationship from different drawings, encounters and feelings.

Teaching conclusion

Understanding connects what a single appearance leaves apart.

08 — Shared concepts and personal experience

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Basis 46–51 and the full 1918 addition, 51–53. Split into two sittings if helpful.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Distinguish conceptual content, its individual apprehension, accompanying feeling and retained representation.

Detailed teaching

Two students draw different triangles. One is narrow and one broad. In plane geometry, both are closed figures bounded by three straight sides. The drawings differ, but the students can understand the same defining relation. Steiner uses the triangle to distinguish shared conceptual content from each person's individual way of grasping it.

This is not a claim that everyone always understands the same thing. Ask each student to explain the relation. If one calls any three crossing lines a triangle, the difference can be examined. Shared words are a starting point for checking understanding, not proof that it has occurred.

Here Steiner calls the inward appearance of thought content intuition. Grasping a conceptual relation differs from feeling enthusiastic about it, remembering a label or having a hunch. Someone may understand quietly. Someone else may feel certain while misapplying a concept. Explain what you understand and examine the application.

Now put a drawing away and recall it. The remembered encounter belongs to your experience. If you attend to the memory, that memory can itself become a percept. You are observing something inward, rather than seeing the paper again. This does not contradict the distinction between present encounter and representation. Chapter 5 locates the representation in experience; Chapter 6 develops its exact concept. Use a verbal recollection if you do not form visual images.

Steiner develops a stronger claim: thinking connects individual knowing with a universal conceptual whole. The triangle helps explain that claim; it does not settle every question about it. In the 1918 addition, he asks us to examine our own assumptions about representations and why he treats thinking differently. Keep separate the argument you can reconstruct and the conclusion you accept.

Activity

Describe the two triangles, explain their shared relation, and note a possible personal response. Then recall one drawing out of sight. Label the present observation, concept, feeling and memory. Finally reconstruct one claim and reason from the addition and record a question about it.

Questions and explained answers

Must the drawings be identical to share a concept?

No; their proportions can differ while the defining relation is shared.

Does excitement establish intuition or truth?

No; it is an accompanying feeling, not the understood conceptual content or a test of application.

Can a memory be a percept?

Yes, when observed as an inward experience; this does not make it a new encounter with the external object.

Teaching conclusion

Distinguish what is understood from how it is individually encountered and remembered.

09 — Human individuality: concepts, encounters and feeling

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: GA 4 Chapter 6, Basis 54–57. Pause after marker 55 to work through the recognition distinction.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Explain an individualized concept, distinguish two kinds of recognition, and describe the personal significance of feeling.

Detailed teaching

You understand what a bicycle is. One afternoon you encounter a bicycle with a blue basket by a gate. Later you remember that encounter. The general concept can apply to many bicycles; your remembered representation retains a reference to this particular encounter. This is the direction of Steiner's term individualized concept. It does not mean that you invent a private truth about bicycles.

The next day you see a bicycle. Two judgments are possible: this is another object of the same kind, or this is the very bicycle encountered yesterday. Steiner distinguishes these in the chapter. The second judgment connects the new encounter with the particular earlier one. A similar blue basket may be insufficient to establish identity; a remembered detail can be mistaken or shared by several objects.

This also clarifies why a concept, a representation and a skill are distinguishable. You may explain what a bicycle is, recall a particular ride, and have learned to ride. Telling a clear story does not by itself demonstrate the ability. In the chapter's account, experience develops through the meeting of perception and thinking; its examples should not erase these practical distinctions.

Now consider feeling. Two students understand the same explanation. One feels relieved after earlier confusion; the other feels frustrated at having spent so long on the question. Shared conceptual content does not require identical personal significance. Steiner distinguishes the particular encounters that shape our representations from the feelings we associate with them.

Think of a fictional teacher who explains a subject carefully and invites a hesitant student into the discussion. Understanding, interest in the subject and care for the student can belong to one response. Brian's teacher examples help bring the chapter's closing connection between knowing and feeling into ordinary life. An enthusiastic manner does not prove an explanation true, and a quiet manner does not imply lack of feeling.

The chapter opens with the broader philosophical claim that self and object belong to a common world process. It closes with the development of knowing and feeling together. Explain those claims in their place in Steiner's argument; the bicycle example makes a distinction understandable without proving the whole philosophy or reincarnation.

Optional expressive activity: Describe the same imagined meeting with the bicycle in two short sentences or two sketches, once as a welcome discovery and once as an inconvenience. Identify what you kept constant and what changed in the presentation. This explores personal significance; it does not certify emotional development. A spoken description is equally suitable.

Activity

Use the fictional bicycle encounter. Write one general concept, one reference to the particular encounter and two possible feelings about it. Then distinguish what would support “another bicycle” from “that same bicycle.” Finally explain how a student might revise an interpretation while acknowledging their feeling.

Questions and explained answers

What does individualized mean here?

A concept retains its reference to a particular percept or encounter; it does not become an arbitrary private opinion.

Does recognizing a bicycle establish it is yesterday's bicycle?

No. Recognizing a kind and identifying the same object are different judgments.

Must two people feel alike to understand the same explanation?

No. Shared conceptual content can have different personal significance.

Does acknowledging a feeling require accepting the interpretation attached to it?

No. One can recognize the experience and examine what it is taken to mean.

Teaching conclusion

Shared understanding, particular encounters and personal feeling are distinguishable aspects of an individual life.

10 — What would a limit to knowledge mean?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Basis 58–60; then 61–67 before the addition; finally the 1918 addition, 67–69. Use separate sittings.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Explain Steiner's argument while distinguishing it from omniscience, certainty and a promise of individual attainment.

Detailed teaching

You want to know how many people attended a meeting, but cannot yet access the attendance record. You have identified a gap in information. This is different from asking whether the meeting was good without specifying what good means. That question may require clarification: attendance, understanding and satisfaction are different criteria. Neither difficulty by itself proves that an answer is impossible in principle.

Steiner's chapter addresses a broader philosophical claim. His monism treats percept and concept as two aspects of one reality that knowing brings together. A theory that puts the true explanation in a completely separate realm must explain how it can say anything about that realm. Steiner argues that it either borrows content from experience or supplies no content for the supposed explanation. He rejects the barrier produced by that account of two worlds.

The book also acknowledges constraints of place, time and individual capacity. A question unresolved today need not establish a permanent boundary to knowing. But it does not follow that every person will answer every question, that all missing records will become available, or that failure to know is laziness. Be precise about both what the argument proposes and what it has not established for a particular case.

The 1918 addition adds another distinction. Extending the range of what is perceived and understanding its connections more deeply are different developments. A longer list of observations does not explain itself. Conversely, a proposed explanation must remain connected to what supports it. The addition's discussion of inferred physical quantities also prevents reducing the chapter to the rule that only what you directly see can be known.

Steiner uses percept more broadly than external sensory content, including what is encountered inwardly or, in his account, spiritually. Understanding that usage helps read later works. It does not establish that a particular spiritual report is accurate. Keep the reported experience, its interpretation and the reasons for accepting the interpretation distinguishable.

Activity

Choose one unanswered study question. State it clearly, identify the information or explanation missing, and propose a relevant next step. If access is unavailable, record that limitation. Then reconstruct Steiner's criticism of the separate explanatory realm in two or three steps. Say which part of his argument you understand and which part you would question.

Questions and explained answers

Does not knowing today prove unknowability?

No; it identifies the current state of knowledge, not a principled impossibility.

Does rejecting that inference guarantee an eventual answer?

No; that would require further grounds.

Are broader perception and deeper understanding identical?

No; the addition distinguishes the range of perceptual content from conceptual penetration.

Does this chapter prove a particular spiritual claim?

No; understanding the proposed possibility and assessing a specific claim are different tasks.

Teaching conclusion

State what is unknown, why it is unknown, and what would justify a stronger conclusion.

11 — Thinking, feeling and willing

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Basis 71–74, then reread the 1918 addition at 74.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Explain the chapter's criticism of one-sided accounts and distinguish active thinking from merely recalling its result.

Detailed teaching

A participant says she cannot see the board. You consider possible seating changes, feel concern and, with her agreement, move a chair. Understanding, feeling and willing are distinguishable aspects of this response. They are not three isolated compartments or a fixed sequence through which every response must pass.

Your concern is part of your experience. It does not by itself tell you which seat will help. The act of moving the chair changes the shared situation, but its occurrence does not establish that the motive was free. Asking, considering and checking connect the response to what the participant actually needs.

Steiner examines two one-sided approaches. One makes personal feeling the sufficient route to universal reality; the other gives that role to experienced willing. His objection is not that feelings or acts are unreal. It is that their immediacy does not remove the need to understand them. In his account, they too are encountered as percepts and require conceptual connections. Read the labels philosophy of feeling and philosophy of will in this local argumentative context.

The 1918 addition addresses a different mistake: judging thinking only by the abstraction retained after it has taken place. Consider remembering the conclusion “change the seat” versus reconstructing why a particular change helps. Working through the relation again is a fresh activity. You do not need to recover the original excitement. The chapter does not require throwing away established concepts or replacing reasons with enthusiasm.

Steiner describes essential thinking in terms of spiritual love and finds feeling and will within it. This is his stronger account of the activity, not a claim that every thought is caring or correct. Our classroom example illustrates coordinated understanding and care without proving that account. It prepares the question for Chapter 9: what makes the motive of an action free?

Activity

Identify the report, interpretation, feeling and action in the chair example. Write two possible motives for helping. Then explain the reasoning for a seating change to someone who has not heard the example. If you only repeat “move the chair,” supply the missing relationship between the problem and the proposed response.

Questions and explained answers

Does feeling concerned establish the correct solution?

No; the feeling matters, while the interpretation and solution still need examination.

Does carrying out an action prove freedom?

No; the origin and character of its motive remain to be examined.

What does the addition distinguish?

Living thinking from the abstraction or result by which it may be misrepresented in recollection.

Is joy the definition of intuition?

No; joy can accompany understanding without being the conceptual content understood.

Teaching conclusion

Work through the relations among understanding, feeling and action; do not substitute one for the whole.

12 — A free deed: motive, driving force and ethical insight

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 9 in full; Basis 75–89. Read in two sittings if useful.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Explain Steiner’s criterion of free action without equating it with choice, novelty or helpfulness.

Detailed teaching

Someone misses a meeting. You could prepare a summary, offer a conversation or respect their wish to catch up alone. What would move you toward one response?

Steiner distinguishes the conceptual content of an aim—the motive—from the driving forces associated with disposition, feeling and experience. A relatively lasting configuration of feelings and mental pictures is the characterological disposition. Having notes and time concerns feasibility. Wanting praise can itself be the pictured aim; feeling pleased now is not the same thing.

His analyses of driving forces and aims are related but are not a table assigning one permanent motive to each kind of person. At the level he calls free, an individually apprehended ethical idea itself moves the person: motive and driving force coincide. This criterion relies on the preceding account of intuitive thinking. Merely identifying an influence or announcing “I chose” does not establish it.

Ethical individualism means realizing individually understood ethical content, not following every impulse. A familiar act may arise from fresh understanding; a novel act may seek admiration. The account includes love for the deed, mixed free and unfree activity, and the possibility of understanding an existing norm and making it one’s own.

Ask the absent participant what would help. Their wishes inform the situation but do not relieve you of judgment. Nor does a successful summary prove a free motive. Steiner’s claim about harmony between free individuals belongs to his argument about shared ideas and individually understood aims; it does not promise that every disagreement disappears. Explain that claim before evaluating it.

Are the notes a driving force?

They are a practical resource; their availability alone does not explain why the aim moves me.

Is a feeling of concern the same as an imagined future reward?

No: distinguish a present feeling from the pictured result.

Is every new or helpful action free in Steiner’s sense?

No. The criterion concerns the determining ethical intuition, not novelty or outcome; mixed motives remain possible.

Distinguish the aim, its motivating ground and the means of execution before discussing freedom.

How can an ethical idea become my own ground of action without becoming mere personal preference?

14 — Freedom with other people: monism and authority

Learning objective

Can we share a world of ideas while acting individually?

Detailed teaching

Two members of a study group agree that a newcomer should have a meaningful opportunity to participate. One proposes a short written summary; the other offers a conversation. A shared understanding need not result in identical deeds. To assess either proposal, they need to learn what the newcomer wants and examine their own reasons. The example illustrates a distinction; it does not establish that either action fulfills Steiner’s account of ethical intuition.

Monism, here, means that percept and idea belong to one reality. Steiner does not explain thinking away as merely material, nor put the ground of action in an unknowable director behind experience. He argues that intuitively apprehended ethical content can become active within the individual. This connects the knowing examined in Part I with the free action examined in Part II.

The chapter contrasts this with acting only because an authority commands or because a supposed hidden power determines everything. That does not make all received advice worthless. An unavailable meeting room creates a genuine obstacle. It does not, by itself, tell you that a divine power rejects your aim. Nor does loving the aim justify ignoring every difficulty. Ask whether another arrangement would serve the people involved. Continuing, changing or stopping requires examination of the actual situation and motive.

The first 1918 addition directly addresses the apparent tension between universal knowledge and individual ethical realization. Steiner regards these as connected expressions of intuitive thinking, not two unrelated kinds of truth. The second asks us to examine the concepts used in an explanation rather than rely only on the label given to a worldview. Reconstruct both arguments before deciding whether they persuade you.

Activity

Does monism here mean only matter is real?

No; Steiner gives both percept and idea a place in the account of reality.

Does sharing a concept require identical action?

No; the first addition distinguishes shared conceptual content from individual ethical realization. Different proposals are not automatically free or equally justified.

Does a closed room prove a hidden command, or that every obstacle must be overcome?

15 — Who sets a purpose?

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Basis 96, then 97–98, then the conclusion and 1918 addition at 99.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

How can something that has not happened yet guide what I do now?

Detailed teaching

You intend to hold a reading meeting next week. The meeting does not yet exist as an accomplished event. What exists now is your representation of it. You consult participants, choose a time and prepare the text because this intended result guides your action. Later the meeting may occur, change or be cancelled. The aim was real as an intention even if the planned outcome was not achieved.

Steiner uses purpose for this relation: a represented result becomes effective in the action that may realize it. The later event itself does not reach backward in time. Thinking about an outcome and bringing it about are different. We also need to distinguish understanding a causal connection from using a represented aim to guide our own conduct.

The chapter asks whether the same account should be applied to nature. A plant’s parts and stages can form a lawful whole without this alone establishing that someone first pictured its result and arranged it as a personal project. In replying to Hamerling, Steiner does not reject natural order. He distinguishes that order from purpose in the sense just defined. This is his philosophical argument, not an experiment establishing the mental capacities of animals.

Steiner also argues against assuming that each person arrives with an externally fixed mission. We can formulate purposes and take responsibility for them. Yet choosing an aim does not automatically make it ethical or free. Ask again what moves you: habit, fear of disapproval, or ethical content you have apprehended for yourself? Other people’s wishes and practical circumstances matter when realizing the aim.

Finally read the 1918 addition. Steiner expressly maintains his spiritual account and rejects projecting human-style purposes onto everything beyond human action. He describes a combined human outcome that exceeds the component purposes. Understanding this claim does not require treating the meeting example as proof of it.

Activity

Write three boxes in chronological order: “Present idea of the meeting,” “Actions guided by it,” “Actual result.” Add a possible cancellation. Explain why cancellation does not mean there was no purpose. Then give one possible motive that would require further examination before calling the action free.

Questions and explained answers

What guides the present action: the future meeting or its present representation?

Its present representation. The future event has not yet occurred.

Does lawful natural development alone establish a consciously represented plan?

No; these are distinct explanatory claims in the chapter. Rejecting that inference does not mean rejecting lawfulness.

Does a self-chosen goal prove ethical freedom?

No; its motive still needs examination. The goal may also remain unrealized.

Teaching conclusion

A purpose is an aim represented and made effective in action; neither achievement nor ethical freedom follows merely from having one.

17 — Moral imagination and technique

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 12: imagination and technique, Basis 100–102; evolution and the source of willing, 102–106; 1918 addition, 106–107.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Follow an idea into a concrete proposal and a workable action.

Detailed teaching

After asking the participant what would help, you imagine sending an adjustable digital copy before the next meeting. You then prepare the file, test whether the text can be resized and check that the participant can use it. Imagining the response and knowing how to deliver it do different work.

Moral imagination gives an ethical idea a concrete possible expression in a particular situation. Moral technique concerns the knowledge and skill needed to realize it within actual conditions. The three questions are: what ethical reason moves me, what particular action could express it, and how can it be carried out? These distinguish aspects of action, not a formula guaranteeing goodness.

A technically excellent file may fail to meet the person’s needs. A caring intention may remain ineffective without a suitable plan. People can contribute different skills: you might understand the need while another person knows how to prepare the document. Collaboration need not remove responsibility for understanding and checking the result.

The lecture’s sketch-and-building comparison helps distinguish an anticipated result from an accomplished one. A memory refers to what you encountered; here you form a concrete picture of a possible response that has not happened. This still uses what you know about the world. Imagination is not creation from nothing, and a compelling picture does not supply technical knowledge or establish a good motive.

The supplied Chapter 12 recording concentrates on this practical distinction and largely skips Darwinism. Read that part in the book separately. Steiner argues that later moral ideas can develop from earlier life without their new content being logically deducible from inherited ideas alone. He presents individual moral creativity as compatible with evolution. This is his philosophical argument, not a modern biological demonstration of ethical freedom.

To unpack that argument, separate three questions: how an idea arose historically, what its content is, and what determines this action now. Discovering the history of a group’s arrangements does not, by itself, supply the particular response to a new participant. Steiner argues that the new content must itself be encountered and understood. He also rejects treating inherited moral rules as ready-made natural laws from which each new deed follows. This is a claim to examine, not permission to dismiss other people’s reasons.

At the end of the chapter, practical freedom to carry out a wish is distinguished from authorship of the motive. A broken printer may prevent delivery without replacing the reason you understood. Conversely, having a working printer does not make a motive free. The 1918 addition at Basis 106–107 returns to observation of willing and makes the stronger claim that organic activity withdraws to allow ideal activity. Attribute that claim to Steiner; the file example does not demonstrate it, and it is not an instruction to suppress bodily needs.

Keep this use of moral imagination distinct from spiritual Imagination in other works. Likewise, a human plan is not automatically a spiritual archetype from Theosophy. The classroom example illustrates ethical realization; it does not establish those further accounts.

Activity

Make three columns: ethical reason, concrete proposal, practical requirements. Use the digital-copy example or another modest study-group change. Add a way to ask whether it helped. For the final reading, distinguish historical development from logical deduction in Steiner’s argument.

Questions and explained answers

Is preparing the file moral imagination or technique?

Imagining this particular response illustrates imagination; knowing how to prepare and deliver a usable file illustrates technique. In practice they may interact.

Does technical success prove a free or good motive?

No. Practical success does not settle the source or ethical quality of the action.

Does explaining the history of an idea settle whether this deed is free?

No. Historical development, the content of the idea and the present determining motive are different questions. Steiner asks us to examine the action itself; evolution alone does not settle its freedom.

Teaching conclusion

An ethical reason needs a fitting concrete expression and the practical means to realize it.

18 — Desire, enjoyment and the value of an aim

Learning objective

Explain why judging an aim requires more than adding pleasant and unpleasant feelings.

Detailed teaching

You practise a piece of music to play for a friend. The passage is difficult, yet discovering its rhythm is enjoyable. If you do not finish, disappointment and pleasure can belong to the same afternoon.

Steiner examines philosophical optimism and pessimism. In his presentation, Schopenhauer recommends quieting the will; Hartmann proposes continued work toward a world process ending suffering. These are distinct arguments, not cheerful and unhappy personality types. At Basis 115, the account of Hartmann differs from the supplied lecture’s summary; use the complete passage when comparing them.

Steiner challenges treating striving itself as suffering. Anticipation, practice and fulfillment are distinguishable experiences. He also acknowledges pleasures and pains that do not arise from a prior desire. A mistaken belief can occasion real pleasure: learning that praise referred to another performer corrects the belief without erasing the earlier feeling. Memory and later assessment may nevertheless be mistaken.

Next distinguish a balance of pleasure and pain from satisfaction of a particular aim. An enjoyable film does not fulfill your wish to play this piece for your friend. A learner may willingly accept effort for that particular goal, reconsidering when circumstances change. This is not an instruction to endure every cost or a judgment about anyone’s worth.

An ethical idea can become something a person wants to realize. Enjoying its realization does not alone make the action selfish, just as intense desire does not make an aim ethical. Reconnect with motive, imagination and technique. The closing argument and 1918 addition concern a will sustained by intuitive thinking; they do not declare every impulse free. Assess this modest project and the source’s reasoning, not the value of your own life.

Does an unfinished practice session prove the whole experience was painful?

No. Enjoyment during practice and disappointment about the result are distinct experiences. Either, both or neither may occur.

If praise was based on a misunderstanding, was the pleasure imaginary?

The belief may have been false, but the pleasure was experienced. A later correction must not silently remove it from an account of earlier feelings.

Can any pleasant activity replace satisfaction of a particular aim?

Not automatically. Enjoying a film does not fulfill the wish to play a piece for a friend. The person can still reconsider the aim and its costs.

19 — Valuing a chosen aim

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 13, desire as the measure of enjoyment, ethical striving and the 1918 addition; Basis 116–124.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Explain why a particular aim cannot always be replaced by an equal amount of pleasant feeling.

Detailed teaching

You want to repair a friend’s damaged book. You enjoy a film too, but watching it would not fulfill this particular wish. You imagine a suitable repair, learn the method and discover it takes patience. The question is whether this repair still matters to you under these conditions. If it risks further damage, consulting someone skilled may be a better expression of the aim than pressing on.

Steiner distinguishes two comparisons. One compares pleasure with pain, like credits and debits. The other relates a particular satisfaction to the desire it meets. A pleasant walk does not satisfy hunger simply because it is pleasant. Likewise, amusement does not replace the repaired book when that is what you wanted. The chapter allows pleasure comparisons where alternatives really are interchangeable for the person.

His fraction at Basis 117 relates experienced pleasure to the needs in question. It illustrates why partial satisfaction is still something and why the same satisfaction can meet a smaller or larger share of a need. It is not a validated instrument for assigning numerical worth to a person. The lecture’s books-written example is a different illustration of achievement relative to an aim; counting books does not measure someone’s life.

The next step concerns obstacles. Steiner argues that the strength of a concrete desire, in relation to what hinders it, matters to whether someone continues. He also says that overcoming difficulty can heighten the value of enjoyment. Examine this as his argument, not a rule that additional suffering always improves a result. Obstacles can weaken desire or prevent action; adapting or abandoning a particular plan does not establish a defect of character.

Now reconnect with Chapter 12. An ethical ideal can become something a person understands and wants to realize. The joy of fulfilling it does not by itself make the action selfish: ask whether the person is moved by the ethical content or uses the deed chiefly to obtain a reward. Equally, wanting something intensely does not make it ethical. A determined person seeking admiration may show persistence without the free motive Steiner describes.

Read the closing qualification and the 1918 addition carefully. Steiner is arguing for the possibility of a will sustained by intuitive thinking, not declaring every impulse free. His account of valuing achievement against one’s own aims belongs within that argument. The course exercise examines a modest project and its reasons; it does not turn accomplishment, illness or suffering into a measure of human worth.

Activity

Return to the book repair. Identify the ethical reason, the concrete proposal and the practical skill. Add the satisfaction sought and one obstacle. Explain why a film would not replace this satisfaction, then describe a circumstance that would justify changing the plan. Finish by explaining why persistence alone does not establish ethical freedom.

Questions and explained answers

Does a pleasurable substitute necessarily fulfill the original desire?

No. It may be equally pleasant yet fail to provide the particular thing desired. Sometimes alternatives are interchangeable, but that must be established.

Does partial achievement make a project or a person worthless?

No. Partial fulfillment can retain value. The discussion does not authorize scoring a person’s worth by productivity.

Does enjoying a helpful deed prove selfishness, or does wanting it strongly prove goodness?

Neither. Joy in realizing an ethical aim differs from using help to obtain a reward; intensity alone does not establish ethical content. Examine the motive and the closing qualification about intuitive thinking.

Teaching conclusion

An aim can matter through its particular content; enjoyment, persistence and ethical freedom still require separate examination.

20 — Understanding an individual

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: Chapter 14, complete: Basis 125–127; also the author’s 1918 footnote to the discussion of women, in the endnotes under Chapter 14.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

[INTERPRETATION — existing instructor teaching] Source paraphrases below remain distinguishable from a fresh [BOOK] extraction.

Learning objective

Distinguish what a category tells you from what you must learn from the person.

Detailed teaching

A new member of your reading group speaks little during meetings. You assume they prefer listening and assign them the notes. When you ask, they explain: “I would like to lead a discussion, but I need the questions beforehand so I can prepare.” Quiet behavior was observable; the preference you attached to it was your inference. Listening gives you something that your first description left out.

The chapter asks how individuality is possible when people belong to families, communities and institutions and share characteristics with others. Here, genus means a general kind or grouping, not genius or exceptional talent. Steiner argues that shared characteristics cannot completely explain the thoughts and aims through which someone determines their own activity. Individuality is expressed within a context; it need not mean living without relationships or influences.

His example concerns women’s opportunities. Instead of deciding someone’s vocation from an assumed picture of what women are suited to do, he argues that the individual woman should be able to determine her direction. In the 1918 footnote he retains this argument against an objection comparing women’s opportunities with men’s restrictions in education and work. Another group’s restrictions do not answer the question of individual freedom. Wanting a role still differs from having its practical skills; examine relevant capacities without substituting a group label for evidence.

Read the opening critically as well. Steiner makes sweeping assertions about racial groups determining characteristics and conduct, and uses historical assumptions about sex and “natural limitations.” The course does not adopt those assertions as established explanations of people. We can identify the chapter’s argument against reducing an individual to a category while also questioning the group generalizations with which it begins. The lecture’s prediction that women will surpass men spiritually is an additional claim, not established by this chapter; it also returns to the kind of group judgment the lesson asks us to examine.

The book then connects individuality with knowing. To understand someone’s freely formed aim, Steiner asks us to receive the concepts by which that person understands their action, instead of immediately replacing them with our own. In the example, ask what leading the discussion means to this member and why it matters. Restate the explanation and let them correct you. This calls for attentive thinking, not abandoning thought, claiming to read minds or agreeing with everything said. Once you have understood a reason, you can still evaluate it and examine the consequences of the proposed action.

At the close, Steiner says that nobody is wholly generic or wholly individual. Freedom develops to a greater or lesser extent within a person’s activity. Do not turn this into permanent classes of superior and inferior people. An inherited practice can also be understood and taken up through one’s own ethical insight; freedom does not require rejecting everything learned from others. Revisit Chapters 9 and 12: the determining motive matters, not novelty alone.

The chapter ends with a claim about community: Steiner locates moral development in individual ethical intuitions and their reception by others. Our group might agree to share discussion questions beforehand after understanding the newcomer’s proposal. This illustrates how an individual contribution can change a shared practice. It does not prove that every personal proposal is ethical, or establish the book’s larger account of the origin of all moral life.

Activity

Make three columns: what was observed, what was assumed, and what the person explained. Use the quiet-member example. Write a respectful question, a restatement they could correct, and a practical way to explore the proposed role. Then explain why both a negative stereotype and a flattering stereotype can prevent understanding an individual.

Questions and explained answers

Does being quiet show that someone does not want to lead?

No. Quiet behavior is an observation; not wanting to lead is an inference that needs checking. The person’s explanation can change your understanding.

Does receiving another person’s concepts mean agreeing with their proposal?

No. First try to understand their reason faithfully. You can then assess it, discuss consequences and disagree without substituting a stereotype for their account.

Must a free individual reject every inherited practice?

No. The closing passage allows inherited social impulses to acquire ethical value when taken up through individual intuition. The source of the present motive matters; unfamiliarity alone does not establish freedom.

Teaching conclusion

A category cannot supply a person’s own reasons. Observe, ask, understand and then assess.

21 — Knowing and acting: bring the argument together

Prior course source: philosophy-of-freedom-consolidated.mjs; prior reference: The Consequences of Monism and both 1918 additions; Basis 129–135. Optional appendix: Basis 136–140. Amrine’s shortened conclusion is a comparison, not a replacement for the additions.. These references retain the old edition/marker system and are not newly verified PDF locators.

Learning objective

Explain how the account of thinking supports the account of freedom, then assess a concrete case.

Detailed teaching

Your study group’s plan worked: a participant can now use the material. Does that result show that you understood the situation accurately? Does it establish that your motive was free? Begin with two separate answers.

The conclusion reconnects the book’s halves. In knowing, Steiner argues that percept and concept together disclose reality; neither disconnected impressions nor abstract concepts alone suffice. He locates this activity within experience and rejects explaining it by inventing an inaccessible realm beyond experience. Shared conceptual content does not mean that all individuals possess the same knowledge or that every judgment is correct.

In acting, he locates the determining ethical content within human intuition and its realization through moral imagination. The first 1918 addition makes the dependence explicit: the account of ethical action relies on intuitive thinking having a self-sustaining character. Otherwise the experienced consciousness of freedom could still be illusory. A successful helpful deed cannot substitute for that philosophical argument.

The second addition describes the relation to Steiner’s later spiritual writings. He regards the experience of thinking as a spiritual experience and the book as their philosophical foundation. He also explicitly says that their particular contents cannot be logically deduced from this book. Keep his proposed continuity and this limitation together. Reading the Colour or temperament course is not required to complete this argument.

Return to the original participation case. Separate what was observed, the conceptual relation you understood, the aim, possible driving forces, the imagined response and the practical means. Then give the strongest objection to your interpretation. Understanding the argument includes being able to identify what would need further support; agreement is not the completion criterion.

Activity

Prepare 300–450 words, a labelled concept map or an equivalent spoken account. Use your opening case, reconstruct the knowledge-to-freedom connection, cite one passage from each half and the conclusion, distinguish intuition/imagination/technique, and revise one claim after an objection. Questions and explained answers

Why does the second half depend on the first?

The freedom argument relies on the account of intuitive thinking as self-sustaining activity. Ethical confidence alone cannot establish that premise.

Can the book’s conclusion be used to deduce the later spiritual teachings?

No. Steiner presents a philosophical foundation and an experiential continuity, but explicitly denies that the later books’ particular contents follow by logical deduction.

What would improve “the help succeeded, therefore I acted freely”?

Separate outcome from motive and its determining ground. Explain the ethical reason, consider habit or approval, and reconstruct the philosophical premise rather than treating success as proof.

Teaching conclusion

Connect knowing and acting through the argument about intuitive thinking; do not infer freedom from a good outcome alone.
