FT-L1 — What is temperament supposed to explain?

Central question

How does the lecture move from individual differences to a theory of temperament?

Learning objectives

Distinguish publisher and lecturer; reconstruct the two-stream claim; explain the developmental and colour analogies without treating them as demonstrations.

Core concepts

Individuality; heredity; reincarnation; analogy; synthesis.

Book material used

FT.01, FT.02, FT.03, FT.04, FT.05. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 1–7 (with the transition to 8).

Background expansion

The edition dates the lecture to Berlin, 4 March 1909. Its biological and historical comparisons are being studied as the source presents them; independent checks are a further task.

Detailed teaching points

FT.01 — Edition framing is a separate voice — PDF 1–5

[BOOK — publisher] The biography calls Steiner a seer and describes his work as a science of spirit, then names practical fields influenced by it. These descriptions frame the intended reader's reception; they are not arguments demonstrated by this lecture. The imprint identifies the translation and revision, while the contents list a single lecture followed by editorial material. [INTERPRETATION] Treating the biographical praise as independent proof of the spiritual claims would confuse promotional framing with evidence. The publication date also differs from the lecture date and the dates of translation and revision.

FT.02 — The general human riddle and the particular person — PDF 6

[BOOK — Steiner] Steiner begins with the diversity encountered among individuals, including within apparently similar types. Temperament is described as an underlying colouring expressed outwardly rather than an exhaustive identity. He then claims ordinary observation cannot fully resolve its source and introduces two contributions to a person: inherited traits and qualities from a prior spiritual existence. The latter are inferred as not adequately explained by heredity. [INTERPRETATION] The lecture asserts that a spiritual history is required but does not systematically exclude all other developmental explanations. The distinction between individual and generic must remain visible when the four types are introduced.

FT.03 — Crystal, plant, animal and individual continuity — PDF 7

[BOOK] A destroyed crystal is contrasted with a plant arising from an earlier plant; animal reproduction adds a claimed preservation of species development, including embryonic recapitulation. Steiner then proposes that a human individual's acquired experience is likewise preserved across lives. The series supplies an analogy of increasing continuity and individuality. [INTERPRETATION] The move from biological reproduction to reincarnation requires an additional premise: preservation of individual experience beyond bodily death. It is not established simply by arranging the kingdoms in a sequence. The embryological formulation is a historical source claim, not a current scientific finding adopted here.

FT.04 — Redi, Bruno and the prediction of future acceptance — PDF 7

[BOOK] Steiner compares resistance to reincarnation with earlier belief in spontaneous generation and invokes Redi as a challenger who allegedly approached Bruno's fate. He predicts that rejection of a prior spiritual existence will eventually appear foolish. [INTERPRETATION] A history of rejected ideas later accepted does not prove that this particular rejected idea is true. The claim about Redi's danger is not substantiated in the lecture, and the analogy must not replace independent historical checking. The passage's function is rhetorical and historical: it portrays current disbelief as a possible limitation of an age's assumptions.

FT.05 — Temperament mediates two streams — PDF 7–8

[BOOK] The practical explanatory question is how a reincarnating individuality unites with a body received through parents. Temperament is proposed as the synthesis in which each stream modifies the other. Mixing blue and yellow to obtain green illustrates a result that expresses both contributions rather than either alone. [INTERPRETATION] This describes the intended role of temperament in the theory; it does not supply an independently observed mechanism of incarnation. 'Stream' and colour mixture are explanatory images. A teacher should preserve the spiritual claim instead of silently replacing it with the modern idea of genes plus environment.

Examples and case studies

Source cases: crystal, plant, animal, Redi and Bruno, and colour mixture. Each has a different function: proposed continuity, historical analogy or image of synthesis. None independently establishes reincarnation.

Important people and events

Steiner; Kelly and Barton as translator/reviser; Redi and Bruno as historical figures invoked by the lecture.

Misconceptions

A sequence from mineral to human logically proves repeated human lives. The last transition requires a premise that the earlier examples do not establish.

Debate or complication

The lecture values individual difference while saying that only spiritual science can fully explain it. Its exclusivity claim needs more support than the mere existence of difference.

Connections to previous lessons

Begin from the source map. Readers of Theosophy will recognize related terminology, but must still examine this lecture’s own argument.

Discussion questions

Which premise would have to be true for the reincarnation inference to work? What does the colour analogy explain, and what does it leave unexplained?

Activity / exercise

Draw the developmental sequence using one box per kingdom. Mark every arrow “observed relation,” “source assertion,” or “analogy.” Then write the extra premise needed for individual continuity beyond death.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: A satisfactory response distinguishes preservation through reproduction from the proposed preservation of an individual’s experience. It does not treat a prediction of future acceptance as present evidence.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 1–7 (with the transition to 8); exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

Redi’s work and the specific danger claim; the lecture’s historical embryology; the differences between inheritance, development and the proposed reincarnating contribution.

FT-L2 — Four members, four portraits

Central question

How do the proposed spiritual members become explanations of temperament and appearance?

Learning objectives

Define the four members locally; reproduce the predominance mapping; distinguish observed behavior, claimed cause and bodily stereotype.

Core concepts

Physical; etheric; astral; I; predominance; bodily expression; physiognomy.

Book material used

FT.06, FT.07, FT.08, FT.09, FT.10, FT.11, FT.12. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 8–10.

Background expansion

The lecture refers to earlier explanations of the fourfold constitution. Its correspondences are presented as spiritual-scientific claims, not as a validated physiological test.

Detailed teaching points

FT.06 — Four members and their roles — PDF 8

[BOOK] The physical body is shared with the mineral domain; the etheric is the life organization joined to the physical until death; the astral bears changing sensations, drives, desires and thought-content; the I bears self-awareness. These are distinct constituents in Steiner's account, not four body parts visible in anatomy. The present lecture invokes prior discussions rather than independently establishing every member. [INTERPRETATION] Students need the distinctions to follow the later correspondence scheme, but learning the scheme is not evidence that the proposed supersensible members have been verified.

FT.07 — Predominance and bodily expression — PDF 8

[BOOK] Predominance of the I-bearer corresponds to choleric temperament, astral to sanguine, etheric to phlegmatic, and physical to melancholic. Bodily expressions are then assigned: blood circulation, nervous system, glandular system and the physical body itself respectively. The claimed relation is not simply 'a person has more blood' or 'a person lacks an I'; all four members are present, differently related. [INTERPRETATION] The lecture states these correspondences and reasons from them, but provides no comparative measurements or diagnostic test establishing them. They must remain an attributed anthroposophical account, not a physiological classification certified by this companion.

FT.08 — Choleric force and the I — PDF 8–9

[BOOK] The choleric is portrayed as forcefully asserting a will and overcoming resistance, with this force attributed to the predominance of I and blood. The account extends into a claim that the I restrains growth of other members, used to explain the relative bodily forms of Fichte and Napoleon. [INTERPRETATION] Assertive conduct is the described phenomenon; I/blood predominance is its proposed explanation; the historical figures are illustrative identifications. These are three different evidentiary levels. Neither the examples nor confidence in the portrayal establish the claimed cause of a person's stature or behavior.

FT.09 — Sanguine mobility and the restraint claim — PDF 8–10

[BOOK] Sanguine experience is portrayed as readily engaged by a sensation or image but quickly drawn onward. Steiner attributes this to astral and nervous predominance, with the I and blood ordinarily exerting restraint. He invokes anemia and disturbed imagery as an illustration of allegedly insufficient restraint. [INTERPRETATION] That physiological and clinical account is not established in the lecture and is not a diagnostic explanation to apply to a child. The teaching-relevant distinction within the source is between difficulty sustaining an interest and absence of any capacity for interest: the educational proposal later depends on the child being able to care deeply about someone.

FT.10 — Phlegmatic inward comfort — PDF 9–10

[BOOK] Predominant etheric activity is said to regulate growth and metabolism while directing attention toward inward well-being. External events are allowed to pass with limited involvement. The bodily portrait includes still expression, plumpness and a loose gait, with fat attributed to etheric activity. [INTERPRETATION] The source links a described disposition, spiritual explanation and bodily stereotype; it does not establish that body shape reveals personality or supplies its cause. The later educational proposal makes shared enthusiasm, rather than direct admonition, the bridge from this inward comfort toward interest in the world.

FT.11 — Melancholic resistance and pain — PDF 9–10

[BOOK] The physical body is described as resisting rather than serving the other members: it obstructs etheric well-being, astral liveliness and the I's purposeful striving. The person allegedly experiences this as pain and despondency. The outward portrait includes lowered head, dull eyes and a heavy gait. [INTERPRETATION] This is a spiritual account of the meaning and source of a disposition, not an established explanation of depression or other illness. Its later pedagogical move depends on redirecting a capacity for suffering toward compassion, rather than simply commanding cheerfulness.

FT.12 — Outward form and limits of the portrait — PDF 9–10

[BOOK] Chiselled versus mobile features, firm versus springing gait, and dark versus blue eyes are offered as contrasts between choleric and sanguine forms. Steiner says that stature cannot be reduced to 'all cholerics short, all sanguines tall': proportion within the total form matters. [INTERPRETATION] This qualification prevents one crude universal rule but does not independently validate the remaining physiognomic claims. It must be taught together with the stronger qualification that actual temperaments are mixed. A student should not use eye colour, weight, gait or height as a reliable means of assigning another person's character.

Examples and case studies

Source cases: Fichte and Napoleon; forceful and springing gait; shifting interest; inward comfort; bodily resistance. Instructor comparison: seeing someone walk heavily does not by itself disclose why they walk that way.

Important people and events

Fichte and Napoleon are attributed examples, not independently assessed subjects. The source’s claims about their forms are not established by naming them.

Misconceptions

Predominance means only one member exists, or visible body shape reliably reveals personality. The account assumes all four members and later insists on mixtures.

Debate or complication

The source qualifies a simple height rule by proportion, but that qualification does not prove the more elaborate physiognomic account.

Connections to previous lessons

Lesson 1 introduced the two streams. This lesson explains the internal relation through which the lecture says their synthesis becomes visible.

Discussion questions

Which statements describe a pattern, and which purport to explain its cause? What additional evidence would be needed to move from a portrait to a reliable inference?

Activity / exercise

Make four columns: source-described behavior; spiritual member; bodily correspondence; evidence supplied. Complete every row from the reading and write “asserted correspondence” where the lecture does not demonstrate a causal link.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: The I/blood, astral/nerves, etheric/glands and physical/body mappings should be accurate and explicitly attributed. A strong answer does not convert anemia, body weight or eye colour into a temperament diagnosis.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 8–10; exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

Independent assessment of the physiological, clinical and appearance claims; primary contexts for the historical figures. These source claims are not used as personal health advice.

FT-L3 — A scheme that must not erase the person

Central question

Why does the lecture reject both pure types and the elimination of temperament?

Learning objectives

Explain mixtures and the value assigned to diversity; identify the historical danger labels; reconstruct the principle of working with existing capacities.

Core concepts

Mixture; diversity; imbalance; capacity; guidance; homogenization.

Book material used

FT.13, FT.14. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 10–11.

Background expansion

The danger vocabulary belongs to this historical lecture and translation. It does not provide a current diagnostic system or evidence that a temperament leads to a particular illness.

Detailed teaching points

FT.13 — Diversity, mixture and the danger vocabulary — PDF 10–11

[BOOK] Pure types do not occur; each person is said to have a basic temperament mingled with others, and Napoleon is also described as partly phlegmatic. Diversity is valued rather than something education should eliminate. Each imbalance is assigned a lesser and greater danger: uncontrolled anger and blinkered pursuit; flightiness and mania; apathy and what the translation calls idiocy; self-absorption in suffering and insanity. [INTERPRETATION] These historical labels and asserted progressions are not a validated psychiatric classification. The pedagogical argument is to guide a disposition without erasing individuality; it does not justify predicting illness from a temperament label.

FT.14 — Work with an existing capacity — PDF 11

[BOOK] The governing educational principle is to begin with what the person already has rather than force in a missing quality. Beating attentiveness into a sanguine child is explicitly rejected. The lecture then searches for an indirect route by which an existing capacity can develop a different relationship to the world. [INTERPRETATION] This reasoning can be explained without silently certifying every temperament assignment: identify the observed capacity, proposed intervention and desired change separately. The claim that only spiritual science can yield genuinely beneficial knowledge is stronger than the practical examples themselves establish.

Examples and case studies

Source example: Napoleon is described as choleric with a phlegmatic element. Instructor example: a child can be quiet in one situation and animated in another; one observed episode is insufficient for an exhaustive identity.

Important people and events

Napoleon reappears to complicate rather than merely confirm the earlier portrait. The educational argument concerns actual children rather than pure specimens.

Misconceptions

Education should remove temperament so that everyone behaves alike. Steiner instead assigns value to diversity while seeking to guide imbalance.

Debate or complication

The lecture’s appreciative account of difference coexists with harsh and unverified descriptions of extreme danger. Both belong to the source and require different kinds of assessment.

Connections to previous lessons

Lesson 2’s portraits become misleading if detached from the qualifications explained here.

Discussion questions

How should a mixed account change the way an observer listens to someone? What separates guiding a tendency from suppressing a person’s individuality?

Activity / exercise

Write a provisional description of the invented child using only the two observations given. List three things still unknown, then explain why the source’s “work with what exists” principle calls for discovering a capacity.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: The answer should preserve uncertainty about identity, avoid clinical prediction and identify a real capacity as the starting point. The source explicitly rejects beating attention into a child.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 10–11; exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

Historical meaning of the translated danger labels; comparison with modern temperament research and the other source lecture without conflating them.

FT-L4 — Four proposed educational relationships

Central question

What is meant to change through affection, respect, compassion or shared interest?

Learning objectives

Compare the four relational proposals; reconstruct their stated reasons; preserve limits and distinguish the source from an instructor’s adaptation.

Core concepts

Affection; competence; esteem; compassion; reflected interest; indirect development.

Book material used

FT.15, FT.16, FT.17, FT.18. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 11–12.

Background expansion

This lesson analyzes proposals in the text. It does not treat their effectiveness as established merely because the explanatory story is coherent.

Detailed teaching points

FT.15 — Sanguine interest through relationship — PDF 11

[BOOK] Affection for a particular person is proposed as a stable focus from which attention can grow. The adult is to find what actually engages the child, allow it to work, withdraw it and later restore it to intensify interest. The proposed route uses existing responsiveness instead of attacking it as a defect. [INTERPRETATION] The passage describes a relational and rhythmic strategy; it does not supply outcome evidence or justify withholding affection as punishment. The object of withdrawal in the text is the interesting thing, not the child's basic security or the adult's care.

FT.16 — Choleric respect and real challenge — PDF 11–12

[BOOK] The proposed relation is esteem for an adult's actual competence and grasp of circumstances. The child should meet difficulties and resistance rather than an effortless environment. The reasoning is that forceful willing can be redirected through respect and an appropriate task. [INTERPRETATION] 'Natural authority' here depends on the child's confidence in the person's worth and skill; it is not identical with a title or arbitrary domination. The lecture supplies no graduated design for safe challenges, so an instructor-created activity must be labelled as an adaptation rather than the source's exact prescription.

FT.17 — Melancholic compassion and the explicit limit — PDF 12

[BOOK] An adult whose understanding bears the marks of lived difficulty is proposed as credible to the melancholic child. Awareness of others' real suffering is meant to redirect an existing capacity outward into compassion. Attempts merely to amuse the child are criticized, but Steiner explicitly says not to carry exposure to suffering too far. [INTERPRETATION] That limit is essential. The passage does not provide a therapeutic protocol, demonstrate a treatment effect or license deliberately distressing a child. The intended educational relation is understanding and outward concern, not the creation of new pain to force development.

FT.18 — Phlegmatic interest through companions — PDF 12

[BOOK] The phlegmatic child is not to grow up isolated. Companions with varied interests provide a reflected route to enthusiasm: their engagement can awaken the child's engagement when objects alone do not. Steiner also proposes directing impassivity toward things for which it is appropriate. [INTERPRETATION] This is more specific than 'make the child active': it changes the social setting and recognizes that calm nonreaction can sometimes fit a situation. The lecture gives a rationale but no controlled evidence of universal effectiveness or a rule for selecting all suitable companions and objects.

Examples and case studies

Source comparisons: sanguine attachment to a person; choleric esteem for competence and challenge; melancholic recognition of others’ suffering; phlegmatic participation in companions’ enthusiasm.

Important people and events

Teachers and playmates perform different roles. The teacher’s competence in the choleric proposal and lived understanding in the melancholic proposal are distinct sources of credibility.

Misconceptions

Withdraw affection to train attention, create suffering to correct melancholy, or equate natural authority with arbitrary control. None is the careful reading developed here.

Debate or complication

The source explicitly limits exposure to suffering but gives no detailed method for selecting its extent. It also supplies no universal outcome measure for the proposed strategies.

Connections to previous lessons

Lesson 3 established the governing principle. Here each proposal turns an existing tendency toward a different relation with the world.

Discussion questions

Why is the phlegmatic route mediated by companions rather than objects alone? How does respect based on skill differ from mere positional authority?

Activity / exercise

For each proposal, write capacity → relationship or condition → intended change → stated or missing limit. Label any classroom scenario you invent as an instructor adaptation.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: Sanguine attention grows through personal interest; choleric willing meets respected competence and challenge; melancholic pain is redirected toward compassion with restraint; phlegmatic interest is reflected through companions. A complete answer retains the limits and evidentiary gaps.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 11–12; exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

Comparative educational evidence; primary source context for child development; how individualized observation might revise a proposed type-based intervention.

FT-L5 — Reason, self-education and the encounter

Central question

If direct exhortation is insufficient, what work remains for reason?

Learning objectives

Explain all four adult proposals; distinguish direct command from changing conditions; connect the conclusion’s social aim with particular understanding.

Core concepts

Indirect self-education; situational design; compassion; boredom; pictorial understanding; love.

Book material used

FT.19, FT.20. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 13.

Background expansion

The source offers brief practices and asserts effects without specifying duration, outcome criteria or responses to failure. This lesson reconstructs that reasoning rather than presenting a therapeutic protocol.

Detailed teaching points

FT.19 — Indirect self-education — PDF 13

[BOOK] Direct self-reproach and argument are said to be weak against temperament, while reason can select situations that work indirectly. The sanguine adult chooses circumstances where short attention fits; the choleric meets situations in which anger achieves nothing; the melancholic redirects suffering into compassion; the phlegmatic deliberately encounters enough tedium to become tired of apathy. [INTERPRETATION] Reason is not discarded: its assigned role is designing conditions rather than issuing ineffective commands. These are source proposals, not established treatments. The lecture asserts their effect without supplying duration, dosage, outcome criteria or a response to failure.

FT.20 — Understanding in encounter and the social aim — PDF 13

[BOOK] The conclusion opposes abstract theorizing about a person to a vivid, responsive understanding through pictures. Spiritual science is claimed to sensitize the soul to both broad patterns and subtle details, allowing a person to bring what an encounter requires. Love is described as the fruit of such understanding, and social life as a succession of particular riddles rather than mere obedience to sermons. [INTERPRETATION] The fourfold scheme is therefore presented as preparation for attention, not an excuse to stop attending once a label is assigned. Its claimed exclusive spiritual foundation is not independently proved by the appealing social aim.

Examples and case studies

Source proposals: tasks suited to short attention, circumstances where anger is ineffective, outward compassion, and intensified tedium. Instructor example: changing a work setting is different from repeatedly telling oneself to concentrate.

Important people and events

The adult becomes designer and participant in the proposed conditions. Another person in an encounter remains someone to understand, not merely a type to manage.

Misconceptions

Steiner rejects reason altogether. He assigns reason the indirect task of selecting conditions in which a habitual tendency meets a different consequence.

Debate or complication

The account of learning through tedium or ineffective anger is asserted rather than supported by reported outcomes. The concluding ideal of love does not prove every preceding explanation.

Connections to previous lessons

Compare the adult’s self-selected conditions with the child’s relationships in Lesson 4; do not interchange the instructions without noting that change.

Discussion questions

What would distinguish an insightful use of a picture from a rigid stereotype? Which outcomes would have to be observed before one could say a proposed exercise worked?

Activity / exercise

Reconstruct one adult proposal without performing it: starting tendency, selected condition, expected experience and claimed change. Mark which stages are explained and which are simply asserted.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: A good reconstruction identifies reason’s continuing role and the missing evidence. It connects the final aim with responsive attention to a particular person, rather than treating a label as the end of understanding.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 13; exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

The full contexts of Steiner’s self-education exercises; evidence for any adapted practice; comparison with Philosophy of Freedom’s account of individual reasons.

FT-L6 — Reading the lecture as a reported source

Central question

What can editorial notes establish, and what remains uncertain in a lecture report?

Learning objectives

Explain the crystal-seeding note; distinguish bibliography from additional evidence; identify translation, report and authorial revision as separate stages.

Core concepts

Editorial qualification; source witness; stenography; translation; revision; audience; bibliographic lead.

Book material used

FT.21, FT.22, FT.23. Read the supplied lecture and editorial material at PDF 14–16.

Background expansion

The publisher states that many lecture reports were not corrected by Steiner and can contain errors. It also quotes his claim that public and member contexts do not imply concessions in his spiritual content.

Detailed teaching points

FT.21 — Notes qualify the main analogy — PDF 14

[BOOK — editorial notes] The notes identify the lecture date and explain that crystal seeding is not intended to invalidate the mineral/living contrast. They identify Redi, Bruno, Fichte and Napoleon and restate the fourfold account, including the I's continuity after death. [INTERPRETATION] The crystal note acknowledges a possible objection but does not demonstrate reincarnation; the reader must still examine the inference in FT.03. The editorial summary is also not a second independent witness confirming the lecture's spiritual physiology. Person-identification notes and asserted mechanisms have different functions.

FT.22 — Further reading is a route beyond this lecture — PDF 15

[BOOK — editorial list] The list distinguishes foundational books, including Theosophy, Higher Worlds, Esoteric Science and Philosophy of Freedom, from related volumes on the soul, life, transformation and spiritual development. Alternate English titles are recorded for several works. [INTERPRETATION] The list helps locate a broader vocabulary and later study but does not supply the contents of those books or prove agreement among every passage. A teacher should not fill a missing argument here by saying it must exist somewhere in a recommended title without locating it.

FT.23 — A lecture report has a transmission history — PDF 16

[BOOK — publisher, quoting Steiner] The publisher explains that translations rely on stenographic and other reports often not revised by the lecturer, so faulty passages can occur. It distinguishes public addresses from member lectures in prior knowledge and the concerns addressed, while quoting Steiner's insistence that he did not merely accommodate members' preferences. [INTERPRETATION] Two claims coexist: intended fidelity to anthroposophy and possible errors of transmission. Neither allows arbitrary correction of an uncomfortable statement. Establish the actual witness, identify uncertainty and seek corroboration before attributing a damaged phrase to either author or recorder.

Examples and case studies

Source cases: crystal seeding as a possible objection; alternate titles in further reading; the public/member audience distinction. These have different functions and should not all be labelled proof.

Important people and events

Publisher and quoted Steiner are distinct voices. Kelly and Barton belong to the English transmission; the reported lecture precedes their work.

Misconceptions

Either every printed word is certainly exact, or any uncomfortable statement may be silently corrected as a recorder’s error. Neither follows from a general transmission caveat.

Debate or complication

The general note does not identify a particular sentence in this lecture as erroneous. A specific correction requires actual evidence from the witness or another identified source.

Connections to previous lessons

Return to the analogies in Lesson 1 and the asserted causes in Lesson 2. Notes can qualify their interpretation without supplying missing demonstrations.

Discussion questions

Why does the crystal note not prove reincarnation? What evidence would justify correcting a damaged statement rather than merely marking uncertainty?

Activity / exercise

Write a short source note naming author, lecture date, translation/revision and PDF locator. Add one substantiated qualification and one unresolved research question.

Explained answer / assessment guidance: The note should identify Berlin, 4 March 1909, distinguish the 2012 edition from its 1987 translation and 2008 revision, and treat other recommended books as further reading rather than material already established here.

Relevant book pages

PDF captures 14–16; exact ranges also appear beside each explanation.

Further research topics

German lecture report and any available parallel records; exact editorial responsibility for each note; source comparison with the other temperament lecture.
